Wisconsin's state section draws from Wisconsin Statutes Chapters 600-655 (insurance generally), Chapter 628 (intermediaries/producer licensing), and Chapter 102 (workers' compensation) — administered by OCI. This material is not a small slice of the exam: PSI's Wisconsin content outlines put Section 1.0, Insurance Regulation, at 35% — 35 of 100 scored items — on both the Life (22-01) and Accident & Health (22-03) exams. Those 35 items cover OCI Commissioner authority, Wisconsin's producer licensing provisions (including the unique 8-hour shared PLE section and no-combined-exams rule), unfair practices, and line-specific Wisconsin law. For Life/A&H exams, BadgerCare Plus (Wisconsin's partial Medicaid expansion at ≤100% FPL — not the full ACA 138% FPL), Healthcare.gov (federal marketplace), LTC training with WI Medicaid-specific content requirements, and Annuity Best Interest (2021 Wisconsin Act 260; effective October 1, 2022) are the highest-priority state topics. For P&C exams, Wisconsin's modified comparative negligence with a 51% bar, at-fault auto system (25/50/10), mandatory UM at 25/50 — with stacking no longer required and anti-stacking policy language expressly allowed since 2011 Wis. Act 14 — and the WCRB (Wisconsin's independent workers' comp rating bureau — not NCCI, in a state whose comp rates sit near the top of the national range) are the core state law areas.

## Wisconsin P&C Auto Insurance Laws

Wisconsin is an at-fault state.

Wisconsin auto minimums (Wis. Stat. Ch. 344):

$25,000 bodily injury per person

$50,000 bodily injury per accident

$10,000 property damage per accident

= 25/50/10

Among the lowest property damage minimums in the country — and well under Missouri, which requires 25/50/25 (Mo. Rev. Stat. § 303.190(2))

Modified comparative negligence — Wisconsin's most tested P&C state law (Wis. Stat. § 895.045):

51% bar: Plaintiff barred if MORE than 50% at fault (>50%)

Plaintiff at ≤50% fault: recovery REDUCED proportionally

Same standard as Indiana (both use 51% bar); different from Missouri's pure comparative (no bar)

UM required:

$25,000 per person / $50,000 per accident bodily injury UM — mandatory (limits set by 2011 Wis. Act 14)

UM stacking is NOT a Wisconsin entitlement — 2011 Wis. Act 14 ended the requirement that UM/UIM limits stack up to 3 vehicles, and OCI's bulletin on the Act states plainly that "[a]nti-stacking language is allowed." Whether limits stack now depends entirely on the policy form, and post-2011 Wisconsin auto policies commonly contain anti-stacking provisions. Exam candidates who learned "stacking allowed" learned pre-2011 law.

Uninsured drivers are a real exposure — the Insurance Research Council put the national uninsured-motorist rate at 15.4% of drivers in 2023, with state rates ranging from 5.7% to 28.2%. IRC reports these figures as percentages, not headcounts.

UIM: optional (not required)

No PIP requirement — at-fault state; MedPay optional.

Statutes of limitations:

6 years for property damage

3 years for personal injury (Different from Missouri, where Mo. Rev. Stat. § 516.120(4) gives 5 years for both personal injury and injury to property)

## Wisconsin Workers' Compensation (Chapter 102)

Coverage: Nearly ALL employers — very broad coverage with minimal exemptions (unlike Missouri's 5-employee threshold)

Public and private employers; part-time employees; minors; family member-employees

Exemptions: domestic servants; some farm employees; volunteers (≤$10/week); religious sect members (certified exemption); Native American tribal enterprises

Wisconsin Compensation Rating Bureau (WCRB):

NOT NCCI — Wisconsin is an independent bureau state (same category as Indiana's ICRB)

WCRB is a licensed rate service organization; unincorporated association of member insurers; regulated by OCI

wcrb.org

Sets rates, rating plans, policy forms, endorsements, and collects statistical data

Wisconsin is a high-rate comp state. Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking study — the standard hazard-mix-adjusted comparison across all 51 jurisdictions — put Wisconsin's index rate at 1.42, or 130% of the study median, 7th highest in the country. Indiana came in at 0.71, or 65% of the median, ranked 45th of 51 — 7th lowest. Note that this is an index against the study median, not an actual average premium; DCBS is explicit that its index is built on a fixed Oregon class mix and is not a state average.

Private competitive market; approximately 300-400 insurance companies approved to write WC in Wisconsin

Assigned risk pool administered by WCRB

Administered by: Wisconsin Department of Workforce Development (DWD) — Worker's Compensation Division; NOT OCI. DWD administers Chapter 102; OCI licenses WCRB and WC carriers.

WC Act adopted: 1911 (Chapter 102)

TTD benefits: 66 2/3% of gross average weekly wage, capped at a statutory maximum that DWD resets annually — $1,296/week for injuries on or after 1/1/2024 and $1,375/week for injuries on or after 1/1/2026. Always cite the DWD maximum in effect on the date of injury rather than a fixed number.

Exclusive remedy: Workers' comp is exclusive remedy under Chapter 102.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- What is Wisconsin's modified comparative negligence standard and how does it compare to Missouri's? Wisconsin uses modified comparative negligence with a 51% bar (Wis. Stat. § 895.045) — a plaintiff is completely barred from recovery if found MORE than 50% at fault. This is identical to Indiana's standard. Missouri uses pure comparative negligence — a plaintiff can recover regardless of fault percentage (even 99% at fault recovers 1% of damages). Wisconsin's 51% bar is more restrictive than Missouri's pure system but more plaintiff-friendly than Maryland/Virginia's contributory negligence (any fault = zero recovery).
- What makes Wisconsin's auto minimum $10,000 PD significant? Wisconsin's $10,000 property damage minimum (Wis. Stat. §§ 344.01(2)(d), 344.15(1)) is among the lowest in the country, and it is far below what several comparison states require — Missouri, for example, sets its PD minimum at $25,000 (Mo. Rev. Stat. § 303.190(2)), two and a half times Wisconsin's floor. A Wisconsin driver with only $10,000 PD coverage who causes $50,000 in vehicle damage faces $40,000 in personal exposure. Advisory implication: Wisconsin P&C producers should always recommend PD coverage well above the $10,000 statutory minimum, especially given the average new vehicle value exceeding $48,000.
- Does Wisconsin allow UM stacking? Not as a matter of law. Before 2011, Wisconsin required UM/UIM limits to stack across up to three vehicles. 2011 Wisconsin Act 14 removed that mandate and expressly permitted insurers to write anti-stacking language, per OCI's bulletin on the Act. Whether a Wisconsin household with two vehicles at $25,000/$50,000 UM per vehicle gets $25/$50 or $50/$100 of available UM depends entirely on the policy form — read the anti-stacking clause before telling a client anything. Uninsured drivers remain a genuine exposure (the Insurance Research Council put the national uninsured rate at 15.4% in 2023), which is why higher single-vehicle UM limits, rather than assumed stacking, are the reliable way to build that protection.
- How does WCRB compare to Indiana's ICRB and Missouri's NCCI? Both Wisconsin (WCRB) and Indiana (ICRB) use state-specific independent rating bureaus — private, non-profit associations of member carriers that set rates, classification systems, and statistical plans independently of NCCI. Missouri uses NCCI (national standard). The rate levels behind those bureaus differ sharply: in Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking study, Wisconsin's index rate was 1.42 — 130% of the study median and 7th highest of the 51 jurisdictions surveyed — while Indiana's was 0.71, or 65% of the median, ranking 45th of 51, the 7th lowest. (Indiana was 4th lowest in the prior 2022 study, so an older ranking may be what you remember.) Treat those as index values against the study median, not as actual average premiums. The differential makes Wisconsin commercial workers' comp a higher-cost but active advisory market.
- What does "nearly all employers" mean for Wisconsin workers' comp coverage? Wisconsin Chapter 102 covers essentially all employers and employees with minimal exceptions — unlike Missouri's 5-employee general threshold or Indiana's 1-employee threshold with construction exception. Wisconsin requires workers' comp for farm operations above certain thresholds, includes part-time employees, minors, and family member-employees. The only significant exemptions are domestic servants, some farm employees, volunteers receiving ≤$10/week, and certain religious sect members. For the exam, Wisconsin has no numeric employee threshold — coverage is effectively universal for commercial employers.
