Are you looking for a stable, flexible, and meaningful career path? If so, you might be wondering how to get insurance license credentials and begin working in a field that truly impacts people’s lives. Becoming a licensed insurance agent opens doors to advising families, individuals, and businesses on important financial protections. Below, you will find a clear roadmap for earning your license, plus tips on preparing for your exam and maintaining your credentials.

By the time you finish reading, you will have an action plan that takes you from zero experience to a licensed professional ready to serve your community. Let’s dive in.

## Understand the licensing basics

Insurance licensing requirements can vary from state to state, but the fundamental system is similar everywhere. States issue licenses based on each “line of authority,” such as life, health, property, or casualty. You can pursue one line or multiple, depending on the type of insurance career you want.

Most states require you to be at least 18 years old, pass a state exam, and go through a background check. Before you move forward, visit your state’s insurance department website to verify the specific prerequisites. This step ensures you know exactly what training hours you must complete and which forms you need to submit.

## Complete your [pre-licensing courses](/prelicensing)

In many states, you have to enroll in mandatory pre-licensing courses before you can schedule your exam. How many hours you owe depends entirely on where you live and what you plan to sell. Twenty hours per line of authority is the most common requirement, but the spread is wide: Georgia dropped to 8 hours per line in June 2025, Colorado requires 50 hours per line, and Florida's 2-20 general lines (property and casualty) license sits at the top of the scale at 200 hours. California is a case of its own — under AB 943 the state retired its 20-hour pre-licensing mandate, and the only education still required before licensure is a 12-hour Code and Ethics course. Whatever your number turns out to be, think of the coursework as building a foundation for your future success. You are learning the regulations, ethical responsibilities, and product details you will rely on every day in your new career.

As you select a course provider, focus on offerings that match your schedule and learning style. Some organizations deliver in-person classes, while others feature online modules you can complete at your own pace. If you prefer interactive lessons and immediate feedback, a virtual live classroom might be right for you. If your schedule is especially busy, then recorded on-demand lessons are a flexible alternative.

Pre-licensing often includes textbooks, practice quizzes, and specialized study strategy. At JustInsurance, we have seen that many people benefit from supplementing official course materials with discussion groups or study buddies, as this helps you keep the information fresh and relevant. Think about which learning methods keep you motivated — a solid study plan now makes all the difference when test day arrives.

## Study for the exam effectively

A structured study regimen is key to feeling confident when you walk into the testing center. Your pre-licensing course sets the baseline, but sustained practice is what cements the concepts in your mind. Consider forming a weekly schedule that covers specific topics in small blocks. For instance, devote one or two evenings to policy types, then spend the next few days reviewing state insurance regulations.

Quizzing yourself frequently will help to identify weak spots. You might score high on questions about general insurance principles yet stumble on state-specific compliance topics. Focus your extra study time on those gaps. Some learners like to create flashcards or short summaries to simplify essential definitions. Others prefer to practice through online question banks that simulate real exam conditions. Whatever works best for you, commit consistently and track your progress.

If you are juggling family, a current job, or other commitments, try weaving mini study sessions into your day. A quick 15-minute review over breakfast or a podcast-style lecture during a commute can have a major impact. The more often you revisit the material, the more easily you will recall it.

## Schedule and pass your state exam

Once you feel prepared, you can schedule your exam through the authorized testing company in your state. Most states use third-party vendors where you can book a time slot either at a physical test center or, increasingly, through remote proctoring options. If you choose an in-person exam, arrive well in advance so you can settle in without any added stress.

Your exam will typically be computerized and consist of multiple-choice questions. The total number of test items often depends on how many lines of authority you are testing for. Keep an eye on the clock, but do not rush. If your mind goes blank, mark that question for review and move on, coming back if you have time at the end.

Many people who have completed their pre-licensing courses report that the exam is quite manageable if they took adequate practice tests beforehand. Remember, it is normal to feel anxious, but confidence grows with preparation. Once you finish, you usually receive a preliminary pass or fail result immediately. Celebrate if you pass, and if you do not, treat it as a learning opportunity. Pinpoint areas of weakness and schedule a retake after extra review time.

## File your license application

After passing the exam, your next step is to submit a formal application to your state’s insurance department. The application usually requires your exam results, proof of completed pre-licensing hours, and fingerprints or other background check documents. You will also need to pay an application fee, which varies by state.

At this stage, you are turning your achievements into an official license. If everything is in order, you can expect your license confirmation and number within a few short weeks. This granted license is what allows you to sell insurance products and provide professional advice.

## Renew and maintain your license

Earning your license is the beginning of a long, meaningful career, but you also must keep up with continuing education (CE) requirements to retain your credentials. Each renewal cycle, you will need a certain number of CE hours. Topics often include ethics, new insurance laws, or advanced product knowledge that benefits both you and your clients.

If you are balancing a hectic schedule, look for [CE courses](/continuing-education) online. Many are self-paced, making them easy to fit around your daily life. Staying current also means you gain sharper skills, which can help you stand out to potential employers or clients. Approach CE as an ongoing professional investment rather than a mere requirement.

## Grow your career in insurance

Once you have your license, you can explore a wide variety of roles. Some agents focus on life insurance, building relationships with families who need help protecting loved ones. Others specialize in property and casualty, advising business owners about liability and commercial coverage. You might choose to become a freelance agent or affiliate with an established agency where you can learn from experienced mentors.

No matter which path you choose, insurance is an industry that rewards your desire to serve people. You will be an advisor that guides clients through important decisions about financial security, so strive to keep learning, networking, and refining your approach. If you ever consider adding more lines of authority, you can broaden your expertise by taking additional courses and exams along the way.
