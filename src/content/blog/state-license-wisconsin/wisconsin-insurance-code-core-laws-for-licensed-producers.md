Wisconsin's insurance legal framework is built on Wisconsin Statutes, Chapters 600-655 — administered by the Office of the Commissioner of Insurance (OCI). What makes Wisconsin's framework most distinctive is the combination of BadgerCare Plus partial Medicaid expansion (100% FPL threshold; 100-138% FPL directed to Healthcare.gov — the only comparison state with this structure), modified comparative negligence with a 51% bar (Wis. Stat. § 895.045), required UM at 25/50 with anti-stacking language expressly permitted since 2011 Wisconsin Act 14, the Wisconsin Compensation Rating Bureau (WCRB) — Wisconsin's independent workers' comp rating bureau (not NCCI) — and the 10 consecutive years of workers' comp rate decreases (2025 marked the 10th year). Add the Annuity Best Interest standard (2021 Wisconsin Act 260, enacted April 15, 2022 and effective October 1, 2022), the LTC training Wisconsin Medicaid content requirement, and the no-grace-period renewal policy — and Wisconsin's insurance legal landscape is more distinctive than its Midwest peer profile suggests.

## Wisconsin Auto Insurance Laws

Wisconsin is an at-fault state.

Wis. Stat. § 344.15 — Minimum requirements:

$25,000 bodily injury per person

$50,000 bodily injury per accident

$10,000 property damage — among the lowest property damage minimums in the country. Do not repeat the old talking point that Missouri matches it: Missouri's motor vehicle liability policy must now provide $25,000 for property damage (Mo. Rev. Stat. § 303.190), so Wisconsin's $10,000 floor stands out rather than shares company

Modified comparative negligence (Wis. Stat. § 895.045): Wisconsin uses modified comparative negligence with a 51% bar:

Plaintiff at 50% or less fault → recovery reduced proportionally by fault %

Plaintiff at more than 50% (51%+) fault → BARRED from recovery

Same standard as Indiana (51% bar) but Wisconsin-specific statute

UM required:

$25,000 per person / $50,000 per accident bodily injury UM

Stacking is NOT a Wisconsin entitlement. 2011 Wisconsin Act 14 ended mandatory stacking of UM and UIM limits, and under Wis. Stat. § 632.32(5)(f) — the "Permissible provisions" subsection — insurers may lawfully write anti-stacking provisions. OCI's Act 14 bulletin states it plainly: "Stacking of Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist limits, up to 3 motor vehicles, is no longer required. Anti-stacking language is allowed." Whether a client can stack is therefore a question about their specific policy form, not about Wisconsin law.

UIM: optional — the insurer must notify the insured that it is available and the insured may reject it; if written, minimum limits are $50,000 per person / $100,000 per accident

No PIP requirement; MedPay optional

Statute of limitations: 3 years for personal injury (Wis. Stat. § 893.54).

Uninsured driver context: mandatory UM exists because a real share of drivers on Wisconsin roads carry no liability coverage at all. Wisconsin does not publish a running headcount of uninsured drivers, and the state-level figures that circulate are modeled estimates from the Insurance Research Council's periodic studies — pull the current IRC estimate before putting a number in front of a client rather than quoting one from memory.

## Wisconsin Workers' Compensation (Wis. Stat. Chapter 102)

Who counts as an employee: very broad — nearly all private and public employees are covered under the Act, including part-time employees, minors, corporate officers, and family member-employees.

Employer thresholds (DWD): Wisconsin does have thresholds, and producers need all three. An employer must carry worker's compensation insurance if it:

Employs 3 or more full-time or part-time employees — coverage required the day the third person is employed

Pays gross wages of $500 or more in any calendar quarter to one or more employees for work done in Wisconsin — coverage required by the 10th day of the first month of the next quarter

Is a farmer employing 6 or more workers on the same day for any 20 days during the calendar year — coverage required 10 days after the 20th day of employment

Out-of-state employers with employees working in Wisconsin must carry it as well.

Exemptions: domestic servants; sole proprietors, partners and LLC members (who may buy in voluntarily); certain relatives of a farmer; volunteers of non-profits receiving ≤$10/week; qualified members of certain religious sects; employees of tribal enterprises; qualifying real estate licensees under s. 452.38

Wisconsin Compensation Rating Bureau (WCRB):

Independent rating bureau — NOT NCCI; NOT ICRB (Indiana)

Wisconsin's own rate service organization; created by Wisconsin law

Regulated by OCI; works closely with DWD

All Wisconsin workers' comp carriers are WCRB members

WCRB phone: 262-796-4540; wcrb.org

Rate trend — 10 consecutive years of decreasing workers' comp rates:

3.2% decrease effective October 1, 2025 (per DWD/OCI joint announcement)

10th consecutive annual decrease — reflects strong Wisconsin workplace safety culture

DWD and OCI jointly approve and announce annual rate changes

Administered by: Wisconsin Department of Workforce Development (DWD) — Worker's Compensation Division; NOT OCI.

Exclusive remedy: Chapter 102.

## Wisconsin Health Insurance

Healthcare.gov: Federal ACA marketplace (no state-based exchange).

BadgerCare Plus: Wisconsin Medicaid with distinctive partial expansion:

≤100% FPL → BadgerCare Plus (Medicaid)

100-138% FPL → Healthcare.gov (marketplace; with PTCs)

Section 1115 waiver (not standard ACA expansion)

ONLY comparison state with this partial expansion structure

No Wisconsin individual mandate.

Wisconsin Healthcare Stability Plan: State reinsurance waiver mechanism holding marketplace rates down — verify current status at oci.wi.gov.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- What is the Wisconsin Stability Plan and how does it affect health insurance advisory? The Wisconsin Healthcare Stability Plan is a Section 1332 State Innovation Waiver that allows OCI to operate a state reinsurance program for the individual health insurance marketplace. The reinsurance program helps hold marketplace premium rates lower by covering a portion of high-cost claims — enabling insurers to price marketplace products more competitively. For health insurance producers in Wisconsin, the Stability Plan means marketplace options may be more affordable than in states without similar reinsurance mechanisms.
- Can Wisconsin clients stack UM coverage across multiple vehicles? Only if their policy allows it. Wisconsin required stacking before 2011, but 2011 Wisconsin Act 14 repealed that mandate and Wis. Stat. § 632.32(5)(f) now expressly permits insurers to write anti-stacking provisions — and most do. Never tell a Wisconsin client that three vehicles at $25,000/$50,000 automatically give them $75,000/$150,000 of UM; read the specific policy's anti-stacking language before saying anything about combined limits. What is still true is that UM itself is mandatory at 25/50, and because uninsured drivers remain a real presence on Wisconsin roads, the limit a client actually carries matters far more than a stacking theory the policy may not honor.
- Why does Wisconsin's 10-year workers' comp rate decrease trend matter for commercial advisory? Wisconsin's 10 consecutive years of workers' comp rate decreases reflect genuine improvement in workplace safety and claims performance across the state. For commercial producers, this trend is an advisory talking point — Wisconsin's declining rates make it an attractive operating environment for employers from other states considering expansion, and the trend may create opportunities to shop accounts for better rates as carriers compete more aggressively in a favorable market.
- What is the workers' comp DWD vs. OCI distinction and why does it matter? Wisconsin workers' comp is administered by DWD (Department of Workforce Development) — Worker's Compensation Division, not OCI. OCI regulates insurance companies and producers (including workers' comp insurers); WCRB sets rates; DWD administers the actual workers' comp benefit system. When Wisconsin employers have workers' comp questions, they contact DWD at 608-266-3046 or dwd.wisconsin.gov/wc. When insurance questions arise about policy structure, they contact OCI or their carrier.
- Which Wisconsin employers actually have to buy workers' comp? Not all of them, and this is worth getting exactly right before advising a client. Under the DWD thresholds, coverage is mandatory once an employer has 3 or more full-time or part-time employees, or once it pays $500 or more in gross wages in any calendar quarter to one or more employees for Wisconsin work, or — for farms — once it employs 6 or more workers on the same day for any 20 days in a calendar year. A sole proprietor with a single part-time employee earning under $500 a quarter is not required to carry it, though they may buy coverage voluntarily. The $500 quarterly test is what makes Wisconsin's mandate reach so far in practice: almost any employee working regular hours crosses it within a quarter, so most employers with even one real employee do end up required to carry coverage. Frame it that way for clients rather than saying "everyone needs it" — the threshold, not a blanket rule, is what the law says.
