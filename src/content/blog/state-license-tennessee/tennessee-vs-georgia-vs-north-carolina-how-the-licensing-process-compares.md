Tennessee, Georgia, and North Carolina share the southeastern geography, significant producer overlap along the I-26, I-40, and I-85 corridors, and substantial cross-border insurance activity. Producers who live near state lines, who represent carriers active in multiple southeastern states, or who are evaluating where to begin or expand their insurance career face a meaningful comparison question. All three states have functioning competitive markets and accessible licensing frameworks — but the specific mechanics of how to get licensed, how much it costs, how long it takes, and what ongoing obligations follow differ in ways that affect real decisions about where to start, which state to add first, and how to manage multi-state compliance efficiently. This post provides the complete, current, side-by-side comparison of every dimension of the licensing process across all three states.

### The Prelicensing Landscape: All Three States Changed Recently

One of the most significant shifts in southeastern insurance licensing occurred simultaneously in all three states — each eliminated or dramatically reduced mandatory [prelicensing](/tennessee/prelicensing) requirements within a 30-month window ending in late 2025.

Tennessee: Eliminated mandatory prelicensing effective March 21, 2023. No course hours required before sitting for the Pearson VUE exam. Tennessee was among the first southeastern states to make this change.

North Carolina: Eliminated mandatory prelicensing effective October 1, 2025 per House Bill 737 (SL 2025-45). Previously required 20 hours per line of authority. Now — like Tennessee — candidates can go directly to the Pearson VUE exam without completing any prescribed course.

Georgia: Reduced prelicensing from 20 hours per line to 8 hours per line effective June 24, 2025. Georgia has not fully eliminated the requirement — 8 hours per line is still mandatory before sitting for the exam. This makes Georgia the most demanding of the three states in terms of formal pre-exam requirements, though the 8-hour standard is among the most accessible mandatory prelicensing requirements in the country.

The practical implication: Candidates pursuing licenses in all three states should plan for 8 hours of mandatory prelicensing for Georgia — completable in one to two focused days — but no mandatory hours for Tennessee or North Carolina. The voluntary prep course remains strongly recommended in all three states given first-time [pass rates](/pass-rates) of approximately 55–65% across the region.

### The Exam: Platform, Format, Fees, and Sequencing

All three states use Pearson VUE to administer licensing exams — a consistent platform across the region that eliminates the interface adjustment that producers face when moving between PSI and Pearson VUE states. The similarities end there, however, as exam structure, fees, and the required sequencing between application and exam differ meaningfully.

#### North Carolina's Unique Application-First Requirement

The most operationally distinctive feature of North Carolina's exam process is that candidates must submit their NIPR application and receive an authorization email from Pearson VUE before they can schedule the licensing exam. This is the opposite of Tennessee's sequence — in Tennessee, you pass the exam first, then wait 48 hours and submit the application. In North Carolina, you apply first, receive an NPN and Pearson VUE authorization, then schedule and take the exam.

This application-first requirement has a practical consequence: North Carolina NIPR applications are valid for six months from the submission date. The candidate must complete all requirements — exam and fingerprinting — within that six-month window or the application expires and must be resubmitted.

Tennessee and Georgia: Both states allow candidates to schedule and take the exam before submitting their license application. Tennessee requires a 48-hour post-exam wait before NIPR submission. Georgia requires a 5-day post-exam wait before application submission.

#### Exam Format Comparison

Georgia's dual-section pass requirement: Georgia's exam structure has a unique feature that catches candidates off guard — the 70% pass threshold applies independently to both the national content section and the Georgia-specific state law section. A candidate who scores 90% on the national section and 65% on the state section fails the exam. You cannot average a strong national score against a weak state score. This makes Georgia's state law section a standalone pass-or-fail component, not just a portion of the overall score.

North Carolina's shorter exam: At 55 questions per exam with a 1-hour-15-minute time limit, North Carolina's exam is substantially shorter than Tennessee's 77-question, 1-hour-45-minute format. Fewer questions means less total margin for error — at 70% required, a candidate needs 39 of 55 questions correct. Missing 17 questions fails the exam.

#### Retake Policies

Tennessee: Unlimited retakes, no waiting period between attempts. Each retake costs the full exam fee.

Georgia: Retakes require a waiting period and returning to prelicensing completion — the 8-hour course must be retaken and a new certificate obtained before retaking the state exam. This is a significantly more demanding retake process than Tennessee's and creates a real cost of failure beyond just the retake exam fee.

North Carolina: A 24-hour waiting period between attempts. If a candidate fails five times or exceeds 90 days from application, they must restart the process — submitting a new NIPR application and completing any applicable prelicensing before retesting. The 5-attempt limit within the 90-day application window creates meaningful pressure to prepare thoroughly before the first attempt.

### Fingerprinting: Three Different Approaches

The fingerprinting process is one of the most practically distinctive differences across the three states — each state uses a different mechanism, a different timing requirement, and a different cost structure.

#### Tennessee

Provider: IdentoGO Fee: $37.15 Timing: After passing the exam; must be complete at least 2 business days before NIPR application Background check: TBI and FBI Additional form: Fingerprint Policy and Acknowledgement Form submitted to TDCI by email or fax

Tennessee's IdentoGO process is straightforward — schedule an appointment online, appear, pay the fee, and the electronic submission is handled by IdentoGO directly. No law enforcement visit is required.

#### Georgia

Provider: IdentoGO (Code: 2TGJ6B) Fee: Approximately $51.50 Timing: After application submission through Sircon Background check: Georgia Bureau of Investigation and FBI Additional requirement: Notarized citizenship affidavit — unique to Georgia among the three states

Georgia's citizenship affidavit requirement is the most distinctive element of the three states' fingerprinting processes. Every Georgia resident insurance license applicant must complete, notarize, and submit Form GID-276-EN — a notarized proof of citizenship affidavit. This requires a separate notarization appointment at a bank, UPS Store, or notary service — typically $10–$20 — and adds an administrative step that neither Tennessee nor North Carolina requires. Online notaries are accepted as of September 2025 clarification, which reduces the friction of this requirement.

Georgia's application submission change (April 1, 2026): Effective April 1, 2026, Georgia resident applicants no longer submit new license applications through NIPR. Applications are now filed through Sircon.com/georgia — the Georgia OCI's licensing system of record. Non-resident applicants can still use either Sircon or NIPR. This change is operationally significant for Georgia residents — the familiar NIPR portal no longer handles Georgia resident initial applications.

#### North Carolina

Provider: Local law enforcement agencies (Live Scan fingerprinting) Fee: $38 (plus any fee charged by the local law enforcement agency) Timing: After NIPR application submission; forms received by email from NIPR Background check: State Bureau of Investigation (SBI) and FBI Fingerprint form: SBI Electronic Fingerprint Submission Release of Information form — must be brought to law enforcement, have law enforcement sign page 2, then faxed or emailed to the North Carolina Licensing Office at Pearson VUE

North Carolina's fingerprinting process is the most complex of the three states operationally. Rather than using a private vendor like IdentoGO, North Carolina requires candidates to visit a local law enforcement agency for Live Scan digital fingerprinting — calling in advance to confirm the agency does Live Scan for non-criminal purposes, bringing the printed form packet, having law enforcement complete and sign the appropriate pages, and then submitting the completed packet to Pearson VUE's NC Licensing Office by fax or email. This multi-step, multi-party process takes longer to coordinate than Tennessee's or Georgia's IdentoGO appointments.

North Carolina's recurring fingerprinting requirement: North Carolina requires fingerprinting every other renewal period — not just at initial licensing. This means every four years, an active North Carolina producer must repeat the fingerprinting process and pay the $38 fee again. Neither Tennessee nor Georgia has this recurring fingerprinting requirement.

### Application Fees and Total Initial Cost

#### Application Fee Comparison

Georgia's application fee structure is significantly higher than Tennessee's — $100 to the Georgia OCI plus $20 NIPR processing for a combined total of $120 per application, regardless of how many lines are included. Tennessee charges $50 per line. North Carolina charges $50 per line plus a $44 base processing fee.

#### Total Initial Cost Comparison: Property and Casualty (Two Lines)

Georgia is the most expensive state of the three for initial P&C licensing — driven by higher exam fees, mandatory prelicensing, higher application fees, and the citizenship affidavit requirement. Tennessee is the least expensive. North Carolina falls in between, with its higher base processing fee partially offset by the lowest exam fees.

### License Structure and Renewal

#### License Validity — The North Carolina Distinction

The most fundamental structural difference between North Carolina and the other two states is North Carolina's perpetual license model:

Tennessee and Georgia: Licenses expire biennially on the last day of the birth month in the renewal year. Producers must actively renew — paying a renewal fee and submitting a renewal application — every two years.

North Carolina: Licenses are perpetual — they do not have a fixed expiration date and do not technically require renewal. However, producers must complete 24 CE hours including 3 ethics every two years by the last day of their birth month to maintain active status. Failure to complete CE results in license lapse. The practical effect is a biennial CE deadline without a formal renewal submission or renewal fee — the license stays active automatically as long as CE is completed on time.

#### Renewal Fees

North Carolina's perpetual license model eliminates the biennial renewal fee that Tennessee and Georgia producers pay. The only ongoing costs for a North Carolina producer are CE completion and the recurring fingerprinting fee every other cycle.

#### CE Requirements

All three states require 24 CE hours including 3 ethics per biennial period with no mandatory classroom minimum. Georgia provides a meaningful benefit for new licensees — individuals who completed Georgia's prelicensing education requirement are exempt from CE for the first reporting period. This gives new Georgia licensees a full two-year period before their first CE obligation begins. Tennessee and North Carolina do not have a comparable first-period exemption.

#### Grace Periods and Late Renewal

Tennessee's 30-day grace period is twice as long as Georgia's 15-day window — a meaningful operational advantage for producers who miss renewal deadlines. North Carolina's perpetual license eliminates the renewal deadline entirely, replacing it with the CE completion deadline.

### Which State Is Easiest to Get Licensed In?

Tennessee has the lowest total initial cost, the simplest fingerprinting process through IdentoGO, the most forgiving retake policy (unlimited, no wait, no prelicensing redo), and the longest grace period for missed renewals. The 48-hour post-exam application wait is a minor inconvenience but creates a predictable, manageable sequence. For candidates who want the fastest and most affordable path to licensure with the most forgiving retake policy, Tennessee is the most accessible of the three.

North Carolina has the lowest exam fees and no renewal fees, making it the least expensive to maintain over a career. However, the application-first requirement — submitting to NIPR before scheduling the exam — is a sequencing reversal that trips up candidates accustomed to other states' processes. The law enforcement fingerprinting process is the most logistically complex of the three, and the 5-attempt limit within a 90-day window creates meaningful pressure for thorough preparation before the first attempt.

Georgia has the highest total initial cost, the most demanding retake policy (failed attempts require returning to prelicensing), the unique citizenship affidavit requirement, and the shortest grace period for missed renewals. However, Georgia's first-period CE exemption benefits new licensees, and the 8-hour prelicensing requirement — while mandatory — is one of the shortest in the country and completable in a single focused day.

### Frequently Asked Questions

I am a Tennessee resident producer considering adding Georgia and North Carolina non-resident licenses. Which should I add first and what is the process for each?

Add North Carolina first — the process is the most straightforward for Tennessee producers adding a non-resident license. North Carolina offers non-resident licenses to Tennessee producers without requiring the North Carolina exam under NAIC model law reciprocity. Apply through NIPR, pay $50 per line plus the $44 base processing fee, provide your Tennessee license certification, and receive the North Carolina non-resident license within the standard processing window. No exam. No fingerprinting for non-residents. North Carolina non-resident licenses are perpetual — no renewal fee ever. Georgia follows the same general framework — non-resident application through NIPR (non-residents can still use NIPR even after Georgia's April 2026 Sircon change for residents), home state certification, $100 application fee. No exam and no fingerprinting for non-resident applicants from NAIC-compliant states. Your Tennessee CE compliance satisfies both North Carolina and Georgia non-resident CE automatically.

North Carolina's application-first requirement confuses me. If I have to apply before I can schedule the exam, what happens if I fail the exam — does my application expire?

North Carolina NIPR applications are valid for six months from the submission date. If you fail the exam, you have remaining attempts and remaining time within that six-month window to retake it — you do not need to resubmit an application after every failed attempt. The application remains active as long as you are within six months of submission and have not exhausted your five permitted attempts within the 90-day exam window. If you fail five times within 90 days, you must restart — new NIPR application, new application fee, and any applicable prelicensing before the next exam attempt. If you simply do not test within six months of your application, the application expires and must be resubmitted. The practical guidance is to submit your NIPR application, receive your Pearson VUE authorization email, and schedule your first exam attempt within two to three weeks of authorization — allowing time for multiple attempts within the 90-day window if needed while staying well within the six-month application validity.

Georgia requires a notarized citizenship affidavit that Tennessee and North Carolina do not. Is this a significant obstacle for most candidates?

In practice, notarization is a minor logistical step rather than a significant obstacle for most candidates. Form GID-276-EN is a one-page document available from the Georgia OCI website. It requires a notary's signature and seal — obtainable at most banks, UPS Stores, FedEx Office locations, and dedicated notary services for $10–$20. As of September 2025, Georgia confirmed that online notaries are accepted, further reducing the friction. The step adds a day or two to the process — complete the form, find a notary, submit with your Sircon application — but does not create substantial difficulty for most applicants. The more meaningful operational awareness for Georgia candidates is that resident applications now route through Sircon rather than NIPR as of April 1, 2026 — the portal change is the more practically impactful recent development for candidates accustomed to NIPR from other states' licensing processes.

Tennessee, Georgia, and North Carolina each offer accessible pathways to an insurance producer license — all three have eliminated or dramatically reduced mandatory prelicensing within the past two years, all three use Pearson VUE, and all three follow the NAIC model law framework that makes multi-state licensing manageable through home state CE reciprocity. The differences that matter most for career planning are the retake policies — Tennessee's unlimited no-wait policy versus Georgia's prelicensing-redo requirement versus North Carolina's 5-attempt limit — the fingerprinting process logistics, and the application sequencing that North Carolina uniquely requires before the exam. Candidates who understand these differences before beginning avoid the process errors that cause delays and additional costs.
