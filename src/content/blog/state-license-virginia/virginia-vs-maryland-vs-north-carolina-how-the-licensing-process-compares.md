For producers in the Mid-Atlantic and Southeast corridor, Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina are the three states most likely to be on the same multi-state licensing strategy. They share borders, serve overlapping client markets, and attract producers who want to cover the full DC-to-Charlotte corridor. All three states now share the same no-mandatory-[prelicensing](/virginia/prelicensing) status, but they still have meaningfully different exam vendors, fee structures, fingerprinting requirements, and renewal rules. Understanding the differences matters both for building your initial multi-state strategy and for advising candidates who are choosing where to start.

## Exam Vendor

Virginia and Maryland both use Prometric; North Carolina uses Pearson VUE. A candidate who has passed the Virginia Prometric exam cannot use that same appointment for a North Carolina retake — they are separate vendor relationships, even though Virginia and Maryland share a vendor. When building a multi-state practice, note that each state requires you to register with, schedule with, and attend a testing appointment managed by its specific vendor.

## Prelicensing Education

None of the three states requires prelicensing education for its major lines today — but they got there at very different times, and Virginia got there first by a wide margin. Virginia repealed its mandate effective September 1, 2002 (Acts 2001, c. 706, repealing former Va. Code § 38.2-1816); only title insurance still carries a separate education requirement, under Va. Code § 38.2-1814.1. Maryland and North Carolina each required 20 hours of state-approved prelicensing education per line until very recently: Maryland's repeal took effect October 1, 2024 (HB 265 / SB 336, MIA Bulletin 24-19), and North Carolina's on October 1, 2025 (HB 737 / S.L. 2025-45). A candidate in any of the three states can now schedule their exam directly, with no course requirement standing between them and test day — though structured prep remains available and recommended in all three given the state-law content.

Because none of the three states requires prelicensing today, the fastest-path advantage for Virginia now comes from its lower exam fee, lower per-line application cost, and straightforward Fieldprint fingerprinting — not from a prelicensing exemption that Maryland and North Carolina also share.

## Exam Structure

Virginia's $35 per-exam fee is the lowest in this comparison. Maryland charges a flat $60 per exam; North Carolina charges $45. Per-exam price is only half the story, though, because the three states do not package the lines the same way. Virginia sells the entire Life, Annuities and Health book in a single sitting — Series 11-01, $35. Maryland likewise offers a combined Life, Health and Accident exam at its flat $60. North Carolina offers no combined exam at all: Life, Accident & Health or Sickness, Property, and Casualty are four separate Pearson VUE exams, each 55 scored questions and each $45. So the Life-and-Health authority that costs one appointment and $35 in Virginia costs two appointments and $90 in North Carolina — and a candidate adding P&C sits one more exam in Virginia and two more in North Carolina.

## Fingerprinting

Virginia uses Fieldprint. Maryland does not require fingerprinting at all — it relies on a non-fingerprint, name-based background check instead. North Carolina fingerprints through local police departments rather than a commercial vendor. None of the three states shares the same fingerprinting process, so a candidate working through multiple state licenses simultaneously needs to confirm each state's specific requirement (or lack of one) rather than assuming a shared vendor.

## Application Fees

Virginia's $15 fee is significantly lower than Maryland's ($54) and North Carolina's ($82) one-time application fees, and the three states count it differently. Virginia charges $15 per qualification per application — *qualification* being the Bureau of Insurance's own term for a line of authority — while Maryland and North Carolina charge one fee per license no matter how many lines that license carries. That structure would start to favor the flat-fee states at volume if Virginia's qualification list were long, and it is not: a full multi-line resident producer holds three — Life and Annuities, Health, and Property and Casualty. The P&C qualification already includes the personal lines classes, so there is no reason to stack a separate Personal Lines qualification on top of it. Three qualifications is $45 — still below Maryland's $54, and well below North Carolina's $82. These are application costs, not recurring ones; the renewal picture is different, and is covered below.

## Score Validity

Exam score validity periods are set by each vendor and can change over time — confirm your specific window directly with Prometric (Virginia and Maryland) or Pearson VUE (North Carolina) rather than assuming a fixed number of days or months if you plan to sit for more than one state's exam.

## CE Requirements

Virginia's single-line CE requirement (16 hours) is lower than both Maryland and North Carolina (24 hours each regardless of lines held). A Virginia single-line producer completes 16 hours per cycle; the same producer would complete 24 hours in Maryland or NC. All three states key the deadline to the producer's own birth month on a two-year cycle rather than a single statewide date — but note what the deadline is for. In Virginia and Maryland it is a license renewal you actually file and pay for. In North Carolina the major-line producer license is perpetual, so there is no renewal filing; the birth-month date is purely a CE compliance deadline, and missing it is what puts the license at risk.

## Reciprocity Across All Three

All three states participate in the NAIC reciprocal licensing framework. A Virginia resident producer can obtain non-resident licenses in Maryland and North Carolina through NIPR without retaking any exam, paying $54 (MD) or $82 (NC) per application. A North Carolina resident can obtain Virginia non-resident authority for $15 per line. The reciprocal process is the most efficient multi-state expansion path — once you hold a resident license in any one of these states, adding the others is an administrative exercise.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Which state should a new producer in the DC metro area get licensed in first — Virginia or Maryland?

For most producers in Northern Virginia, getting licensed in Virginia first is still the logical starting point: a $35 exam fee, $15 per-line application fee, and Fieldprint fingerprinting that is straightforward and affordable, plus no fingerprinting delay to coordinate on the Maryland side. Once the Virginia license is active, adding a Maryland non-resident license through NIPR takes one application and $54 — no exam required, and Maryland has no fingerprinting step to add either. Starting with Virginia and adding Maryland non-resident authority is typically faster and cheaper than starting resident in Maryland, mainly because of Virginia's lower fees rather than any prelicensing difference — both states now skip prelicensing entirely. For producers who are actually Maryland residents, the calculation reverses — start in Maryland (resident requirement) and add Virginia non-resident authority.

Do Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina have identical CE reciprocity for non-resident producers?

Non-resident producers in Virginia satisfy CE requirements by being compliant in their home state — Virginia accepts home-state CE compliance without requiring Virginia-specific hours. Maryland and North Carolina have similar non-resident CE frameworks, though specific requirements for specialty lines (LTC, annuity suitability) may require state-specific training in each state regardless of non-resident status. Virginia's LTC Partnership training requirement (14VAC5-200-205) is a minimum 8-hour initial course — 2 of those 8 hours must be Virginia-specific content, not 2 hours on top of the 8 — followed by at least 4 hours of ongoing training every 24 months, which also carries 2 hours of Virginia-specific content. That requirement applies to non-resident producers selling LTC Partnership products in Virginia regardless of home state CE compliance. Confirm specialty line training requirements for each state individually before selling those products.

How does Virginia's exam difficulty compare to Maryland and North Carolina?

Virginia and Maryland both use a raw 70% passing standard, but North Carolina does not: its Pearson VUE exams report a scaled score, and passing is a scaled 70 — which, per Pearson VUE's own candidate handbook, is neither the number nor the percentage of questions you answered correctly. On top of that, the three states do not test the same lines the same way. Virginia's combined Life, Annuities & Health exam (Series 11-01) has 140 scored questions in one 150-minute sitting, and Maryland's Prometric combined Life, Health and Accident exam covers a comparable content volume. North Carolina splits the same ground: there is no combined exam, so a candidate sits a 55-question Life exam and a separate 55-question Accident & Health or Sickness exam, scheduled and paid for separately. Per sitting, North Carolina's exams are shorter; in total, the content load across the two is broadly comparable, because the national general content (approximately 60–70% of most exams) is the same everywhere. The state-law sections differ — Virginia's state law content covers the SCC's Bureau of Insurance structure, Virginia's auto insurance changes (50/100/25 minimums, UIM stacking), and Virginia-specific unfair trade practices. Maryland and NC have their own state-law sections covering their respective regulatory frameworks. Candidates who prepare using state-specific study materials consistently outperform those relying on national-only content.

Can I hold resident licenses in both Virginia and Maryland simultaneously?

No. You can only hold one state's resident license at a time. If you are a Virginia resident, you hold a Virginia resident license and non-resident licenses in other states (Maryland, NC, etc.). If you are a Maryland resident who moves to Virginia, you must surrender your Maryland resident license within 90 days and apply for a Virginia resident license in the same lines to maintain the no-exam transfer benefit. You cannot hold two states' resident licenses simultaneously — the NAIC licensing framework treats each producer as having exactly one home state.

Which state has the most favorable renewal requirements for a dual-line producer?

It depends on whether you are counting hours or dollars, and the two answers point to different states.

On CE hours, Virginia is the lightest for single-line producers: a Virginia producer holding a Life & Annuities license, a Health license, or both completes 16 hours per biennium (Va. Code § 38.2-1866), while Maryland and North Carolina require 24 hours regardless of lines held. A Virginia producer who also holds a license in a different category — Property & Casualty, for example — completes 24 hours, the same as MD and NC.

On dollars, North Carolina is the lightest: NC's major-line producer licenses are perpetual, so there is no renewal application and no renewal fee at all. (The $82 often quoted for North Carolina is its one-time initial application fee, not a recurring cost.) Virginia charges $10 per line of authority every two years. Maryland charges $69 to renew — a $54 renewal fee plus a $15 fraud prevention fee. All three run off the producer's own birth month rather than a shared statewide date, so the deadline is predictable everywhere; it is simply a renewal deadline in Virginia and Maryland and a CE compliance deadline in North Carolina.

Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina each work for different producers depending on residency, market focus, and career stage. Virginia's low fees make it the cheapest path to a first license in the region, now that all three states have eliminated prelicensing. Maryland's and North Carolina's single application fee structures have their own advantages for multi-line producers, and North Carolina's perpetual licenses remove the renewal fee entirely once you are in. The best multi-state strategy is usually to get your resident state license right and add the others through reciprocity.
