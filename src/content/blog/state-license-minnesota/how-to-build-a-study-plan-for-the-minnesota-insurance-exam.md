A study plan is not a schedule. A schedule tells you when to study. A study plan tells you what to study, in what order, at what depth, and how to verify that you are retaining what you have covered before you sit for the PSI exam. Most candidates who fail the Minnesota insurance exam on their first attempt did not fail because they did not study — they failed because they studied without a plan, covered everything at the same depth, spent too much time on topics that generate few exam questions, and underallocated time to Minnesota state law, which is where the exam separates prepared candidates from unprepared ones. This post builds the study plan that changes that outcome.

### The Foundation: Understanding What You Are Preparing For

Before building a study plan, understand the exam you are preparing for. The Minnesota PSI insurance exam has two distinct content sections — a general section and a Minnesota state-specific section — and your study time should be allocated between them based on where exam questions actually come from, not based on which material feels more comfortable or more interesting.

The general section covers foundational insurance principles that apply across all states. For most candidates entering the insurance market from adjacent professional backgrounds — finance, real estate, healthcare, human resources, construction — much of this content aligns with concepts they already have some familiarity with. The study investment needed here is real but manageable.

The state-specific section covers Minnesota insurance law, statutes, and regulations that are unique to this state. This material has no familiarity advantage — it requires deliberate study of specific statutory provisions, specific numbers, specific definitions, and specific regulatory procedures that you will not find in your general professional background. This is where the exam is hardest for most candidates and where targeted study time pays the highest return.

The content outline: PSI publishes the content outline for each Minnesota insurance exam in its Candidate Information Bulletin. Your [prelicensing](/minnesota/prelicensing) course is built to this outline. Treat the content outline as your master checklist — every topic on it is a potential source of exam questions, and every topic you have not studied is a gap that could cost you points.

### Step 1: Know Your Timeline

The first element of a study plan is an honest assessment of how much time you have between now and your PSI exam date. Different timelines require different study intensities.

Accelerated timeline (1–2 weeks): Intensive daily study of 3–4 hours per day. Appropriate for candidates who have a professional deadline — a job offer that requires licensure within a specific period, a carrier start date, or an agency that needs the producer appointed quickly. This timeline is achievable but leaves no buffer for comprehension gaps.

Standard timeline (3–4 weeks): Study sessions of 1.5–2.5 hours per day, five to six days per week. The most common effective timeline. Provides enough spacing between study sessions for material to consolidate in memory, and enough buffer to revisit weak areas before the exam.

Extended timeline (5–8 weeks): Study sessions of 1–2 hours per day, three to four days per week. Appropriate for candidates balancing full-time employment, family responsibilities, or other significant commitments alongside licensing study. Requires more deliberate note-taking and periodic review to prevent forgetting material covered early in the timeline.

The minimum viable study time: Regardless of your timeline, completing the full 20-hour prelicensing course is the baseline — not the ceiling. The prelicensing course satisfies the statutory requirement and introduces the material; it does not independently produce exam readiness. Practice exams, active review of Minnesota law, and targeted study of weak areas are additional investments that the 20-hour requirement does not dictate but that consistent first-attempt passing requires.

### Step 2: Allocate Study Time by Exam Section

Once you know your total available study time, allocate it deliberately. A rough allocation that works for most Minnesota insurance exam candidates:

General content (50–55% of study time): Covers policy types, insurance terms and concepts, policy provisions and contract law, underwriting principles, and the mechanics of how insurance products work. This is the content your prelicensing course covers thoroughly.

Minnesota state law (35–40% of study time): Covers producer licensing statutes (Chapter 60K), Minnesota auto insurance law (Chapter 65B — no-fault, 30/60/10, PIP, UM/UIM), workers' compensation requirements, unfair trade practices (Minn. Stat. §72A.20), the Department of Commerce structure, MNsure and Minnesota health programs, and Minnesota-specific insurance regulations.

Practice exams and review (10–15% of study time): Taking full-length practice exams under timed conditions and reviewing every incorrect answer thoroughly.

The most common study plan error is inverting this allocation — spending 70% of time on general content because it is more familiar and only 30% on state law because it feels like less material. State law is less material in volume but generates a proportionally significant share of exam questions. Study it accordingly.

### Step 3: Structure Your Study Sessions

Effective study sessions for a high-stakes exam are not passive reading sessions. They are active engagement with material that requires retrieval, application, and self-testing.

Session structure that works:

Opening (5 minutes): Brief review of what you covered in the previous session. Do not re-read — attempt to recall the key points from memory. This active recall reinforces retention better than any re-reading.

New content (45–60 minutes): Cover new material from your prelicensing course or study notes. As you cover each topic, stop and ask yourself: what is the exam likely to ask about this? Write down the key numbers, definitions, and rules — particularly any specific statutory provisions or numerical thresholds.

[Practice questions](/minnesota/practice-exam) (20–30 minutes): After covering new content, complete 15–25 practice questions specifically on the topics you just studied. Do not wait until you have covered everything to start practice questions — immediate post-study practice testing is the most effective retention tool available.

Review wrong answers (10–15 minutes): For every practice question you answered incorrectly, identify why you got it wrong. Was it a factual gap (you did not know the rule)? A reading error (you misread the question)? A distractor confusion (you chose the plausible-but-wrong answer)? Each type of error has a different remedy.

Closing summary (5 minutes): Write two to five bullet points summarizing the most important things you covered in the session. This becomes your review material for the next session's opening.

Session length: 60–90 minutes per session is the effective range for most candidates. Beyond 90 minutes, focus degrades and retention drops. Two 75-minute sessions in a day are more effective than a single 3-hour session.

Rest days: Build at least one rest day per week into your plan. Cognitive consolidation — the process by which studied material moves from working memory to long-term memory — happens partly during rest. Candidates who study seven days per week without breaks consistently report diminishing returns in the final days before their exam.

### Step 4: The Minnesota State Law Study Module

Because the state law section is where most first-attempt failures occur, it deserves its own focused study module within your overall plan. The following topics require specific, deliberate memorization — not just general familiarity.

Minnesota producer licensing (Chapter 60K):

Prelicensing: 20 hours per line of authority (Minn. Stat. §60K.36 Subd. 4)

Certification exam: 70% passing score, proctored by disinterested third party

Certificate of completion: never expires

Exam results: valid for 3 years

Application fee: $50 per line of authority

Renewal: last day of birth month, biennial

CE: 24 hours biennial, 3 hours ethics, 12 hours classroom minimum, 12 hours non-company-sponsored minimum

Lapsed license reinstatement: 12-month window, double renewal fee penalty

Appointment requirement: required before transacting business (Minn. Stat. §60K.49)

Minnesota auto insurance — no-fault (Minn. Stat. §65B.49):

Minnesota is a no-fault state

Minimum liability: 30/60/10 ($30,000/$60,000 bodily injury, $10,000 property damage — lowest PD minimum in the U.S.)

Mandatory PIP: $40,000 ($20,000 medical + $20,000 non-medical including lost wages at 85% capped at $500/week)

PIP pays regardless of fault; six-month deadline to file PIP claim

Mandatory UM/UIM: $25,000/$50,000 (both UM and UIM required)

Tort threshold to sue for pain and suffering: medical expenses exceeding $4,000 (excluding diagnostic tests), permanent injury, disfigurement, or death

SR-22 certificate of financial responsibility: required for certain violations

Minnesota workers' compensation:

Threshold: 1 employee triggers mandatory coverage (no minimum)

Competitive market — not monopolistic; Minnesota Workers' Compensation Assigned Risk Plan is insurer of last resort

Penalty for non-coverage: up to $1,000 per employee per week

Additional penalty: 65% surcharge on benefits if claim occurs while uninsured

Maximum weekly benefit: $1,536.84 (effective October 1, 2025)

Benefits: two-thirds of average weekly wage; unlimited medical treatment

Administering agency: Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (DLI) for claims; Department of Commerce for carrier regulation

Minnesota unfair trade practices (Minn. Stat. §72A.20):

Misrepresentation and false advertising

Defamation

Boycott, coercion, and intimidation

Unfair discrimination

Rebating (both offering and accepting)

Unfair claims settlement practices (frequency standard or willful)

Know the distinction between misrepresentation and twisting (replacement using misrepresentation)

Minnesota Department of Commerce:

Address: 85 7th Place East, Suite 280, St. Paul, MN 55101

Phone: (651) 539-1599 / 800-657-3978 (in-state)

Commissioner holds full regulatory authority

Minnesota Insurance Guaranty Association: P&C guaranty fund (Minn. Stat. §60C)

Minnesota Life and Health Guaranty Association: Life/A&H guaranty fund (Minn. Stat. §61B)

Minnesota health insurance:

MNsure: Minnesota's state-based health insurance exchange

Medical Assistance: Minnesota's Medicaid program

MinnesotaCare: Minnesota's Basic Health Program (BHP) for incomes up to 200% FPL — unique to Minnesota; provides coverage below the marketplace income threshold

### Step 5: Build Your Personal Reference Sheet

A reference sheet is a single-page (or two-page) condensed summary of the most testable facts in the Minnesota state law section — the numbers, definitions, and procedures that are most likely to appear as exam questions. Building this sheet yourself is more valuable than using a pre-made reference card, because the act of deciding what to include reinforces your understanding of what is important.

What belongs on your reference sheet:

All numerical thresholds: PIP $40,000, UM/UIM $25,000/$50,000, liability 30/60/10, tort threshold $4,000, CE 24 hours/3 ethics/12 classroom/12 non-sponsored, renewal fee $80, lapse reinstatement penalty double fee, workers' comp penalty $1,000/week plus 65%

All key statute numbers: Chapter 60K (licensing), Chapter 65B (auto), §72A.20 (unfair trade practices), §60C (P&C guaranty), §61B (L&H guaranty)

The no-fault claim sequence: PIP first, then at-fault driver's liability after PIP exhausted, then UM/UIM if driver is uninsured or underinsured

The tort threshold triggers

The workers' compensation benefit formula: two-thirds of average weekly wage

How to use the reference sheet: Review it at the beginning of each study session. Add to it as you discover additional testable facts. In the final 48 hours before the exam, read through it multiple times — this is the material most likely to appear in questions you can answer correctly with direct recall.

### Step 6: Full-Length Practice Exams

Practice exams serve two functions: they simulate the test day experience under time pressure, and they identify specific knowledge gaps that targeted review can close before the real exam.

When to take practice exams: Take your first full-length practice exam after completing approximately 60% of your study plan. This first practice exam is diagnostic — it tells you where you stand and which areas need the most additional work. Take your second practice exam after completing your full study plan, within 48–72 hours of your scheduled PSI exam.

How to take practice exams: Simulate actual exam conditions. Set a timer for the full allotted exam time (2 hours for 85-question exams, 3 hours for combined exams). Take the exam in a quiet room without interruption. Do not use notes, your reference sheet, or your prelicensing course during the practice exam — answer every question from memory, exactly as you will on the real exam.

How to review practice exams: After completing the practice exam, review every question — not just the ones you got wrong. For questions you answered correctly, confirm that you knew the answer for the right reason and were not guessing. For questions you answered incorrectly, identify the specific knowledge gap and add the correct information to your reference sheet. Spend 30–45 minutes in review for every 85-question practice exam.

Target score for practice exams: Aim for 80% or higher on full-length practice exams before sitting for the real exam. A candidate who consistently scores 80% on realistic practice exams is well-positioned to exceed the 70% passing threshold on the real exam, accounting for some variance in question difficulty between practice and actual exams.

### Step 7: The Final 48 Hours

How you spend the 48 hours before your PSI exam matters as much as how you spent the preceding weeks.

48 hours before:

Complete your second full-length practice exam if not already done

Review the diagnostic results and revisit any areas below 70% correct

Read through your reference sheet completely

Confirm your exam appointment, test center address or PSI Bridge setup, and what to bring

24 hours before:

Do not study new material. Review only your reference sheet and any notes you have flagged as important

Confirm your PSI Bridge technical setup if testing remotely — run the compatibility check again, confirm your Chrome browser is updated

Locate your Certificate of Completion and government-issued photo ID and place them where you will not forget them

Eat normally, exercise if that is part of your routine, and get to bed at a reasonable hour

Morning of the exam:

Eat a normal meal — do not skip breakfast or eat more than usual

For in-person: leave with enough time to arrive 30 minutes early even accounting for traffic or parking

For remote: log in 30 minutes before your reservation time; clear your workspace completely

Brief review of your reference sheet only — no new material

### Frequently Asked Questions

How many practice questions should I complete before my exam?

There is no single correct number, but candidates who complete 300–500 practice questions before their PSI exam — across their full study period — consistently report higher first-attempt [pass rates](/pass-rates) than those who complete fewer. The key is not the quantity alone but the quality of the review after each practice session. A candidate who completes 200 questions and thoroughly analyzes every wrong answer will outperform a candidate who completes 500 questions and moves on without reviewing mistakes. I'm studying for both the P&C and Life and A&H exams simultaneously. Should I study all four lines at once or sequentially?

Sequential is generally more effective than simultaneous. Complete your study and sit for one combined exam before beginning serious preparation for the other. For most candidates, completing the P&C exam first and then the Life and A&H exam works well — the P&C content is more concrete and mechanical, making it easier to build momentum before the more conceptual Life and A&H material. The exception is if your job requires both licenses within a very tight timeframe — in that case, study both simultaneously but segment your daily sessions by line (P&C content in the morning, Life and A&H in the afternoon) rather than mixing content from both lines in a single session.

I failed my first attempt. How should I adjust my study plan for the retake?

Start with your score report's diagnostic breakdown — it identifies which content areas you underperformed in. Do not re-study everything equally; focus your retake preparation on the specific areas the diagnostic flagged. If your weakest areas are in the state law section, dedicate 60–70% of your retake study time to Minnesota-specific law. Rebuild your reference sheet with a fresh eye — the act of rewriting it from scratch often surfaces connections you missed the first time. Take at least one full-length practice exam under timed conditions before the retake, and schedule your retake at least one week after receiving your diagnostic results to give yourself adequate focused preparation time. The 24-hour waiting period is the minimum — most candidates benefit from more than 24 hours between their failed attempt and their retake.

Is it better to study every day or take breaks between sessions?

Taking at least one full rest day per week produces better results than studying every day without exception. Cognitive consolidation — the biological process by which recently studied information is integrated into long-term memory — is partially dependent on rest, particularly sleep. Candidates who study intensely seven days per week without breaks often report that material feels increasingly blurry in the days immediately before their exam. A schedule of five to six active study days per week with one to two lighter or rest days produces more durable retention than continuous daily study of the same intensity.

A study plan that allocates time deliberately between general content and Minnesota state law, structures sessions around active engagement rather than passive reading, builds a personal reference sheet of testable facts, and includes full-length practice exams under realistic conditions is the plan most likely to produce a first-attempt pass on the Minnesota PSI insurance exam. The content is learnable — the 70% passing standard is achievable for every candidate who prepares with the right structure.
