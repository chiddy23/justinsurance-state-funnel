Virginia's no-[prelicensing](/virginia/prelicensing) requirement puts every candidate in the same position: you are responsible for your own preparation, and the structure of that preparation is entirely up to you. This is either an advantage or a liability depending on how you approach it. Candidates who build a deliberate, timeline-driven study plan pass at significantly higher rates than those who study randomly or review casually and hope for the best. This post gives you a working study plan framework — covering what to study, in what sequence, how to measure readiness, and how to calibrate your schedule to the Virginia exam's specific structure.

## Understand the Exam Before You Study

Before you open any study material, understand exactly what you are preparing for. Virginia's licensing exams test two distinct types of content:

National/general content covers insurance principles, policy mechanics, coverage structures, and federal regulations that apply across all states. For the P&C exam, this section has 100 scored questions. For the Life, Annuities & Health exam, also 100 scored questions. This material is the same core content tested in every state.

Virginia state law content covers Title 38.2 of the Code of Virginia, the SCC Bureau of Insurance's regulatory framework, Virginia-specific auto insurance rules (50/100/25 minimums, UIM stacking, mandatory coverage), unfair trade practices, producer licensing rules, and Virginia-specific health and life insurance statutes. The P&C exam has 35 scored state law questions; the L/A/H exam has 40. You must score 70% or higher on each section separately.

Your study plan must cover both. Candidates who under-prepare the state law section fail despite strong national content knowledge.

Your first action: Download the Prometric content outline for your specific exam series at prometric.com/virginia/insurance. This document lists every topic tested by section and percentage weight. It is the authoritative study guide — everything on the outline is fair game, nothing off it will appear.

## A 4-Week Study Framework (Adaptable to Your Timeline)

### Week 1: National Content Foundation

Focus entirely on national/general insurance content. Work through the full content outline section by section:

For P&C candidates: Insurance regulation, general insurance concepts, property basics, dwelling policies, homeowners forms, auto insurance (national principles), commercial package policies, businessowners policies, workers' compensation, and other coverages. Do not rush through categories — work until you can explain each concept without looking at notes.

For L/A/H candidates: Types of life policies, policy riders and provisions, application/underwriting/delivery, individual health insurance basics, disability income and related products, medical plans and managed care, group health, dental, senior and special-needs insurance (Medicare supplement, LTC), and federal tax considerations.

End of week 1 benchmark: Take a full-length timed national-content practice exam. Target score: 70%+. If you are below 65%, extend Week 1 before moving to Week 2.

### Week 2: Virginia State Law Deep Dive

Shift entirely to Virginia-specific content. Work through:

The SCC Bureau of Insurance structure — Title 38.2 of the Code of Virginia, how the SCC differs from a standalone department of insurance, what the Commissioner does and does not do (Virginia's "Commissioner" equivalent is the SCC itself)

Producer licensing requirements: age, residency, exam, fingerprinting, application, fees, CE obligations

Unfair trade practices and unfair claim settlement practices under Virginia law

Virginia auto insurance law: 50/100/25 minimums (effective January 1, 2025), mandatory coverage requirement (effective July 1, 2024), UIM stacking (effective July 1, 2023 under Va. Code § 38.2-2206), FR-44 DUI requirements (100/200/50)

Virginia health insurance law: individual mandate provisions, Get Covered Virginia exchange, COBRA and continuation, small employer health insurance requirements

Virginia life insurance law: replacement regulations, free-look periods, grace periods, incontestability, policyholder rights

Virginia workers' compensation: employer obligations, no-fault framework, Bureau jurisdiction

End of week 2 benchmark: Take a full-length Virginia state law practice exam or work through state-specific practice questions. Target score: 70%+.

### Week 3: Integration and Weak-Area Targeting

Review your practice exam diagnostics from weeks 1 and 2. Identify the specific content areas where you scored below 70% and target those specifically. Do not re-study areas where you are already strong.

Take two full-length integrated practice exams — covering both national and state sections — under timed conditions. For the P&C exam, that means 135 minutes for 145 questions. For the L/A/H exam, 150 minutes for 150 questions. Do not allow yourself more time than the real exam provides.

End of week 3 benchmark: Consistently scoring 75%+ on both sections on practice exams. If you are not there, extend week 3 before proceeding.

### Week 4: Refinement and Final Preparation

Continue targeted weak-area review. Reduce new content study and increase practice exam repetition. Your final 3–5 days should primarily be practice exams and review of diagnostic reports.

Do not schedule the real exam until your practice scores are consistently at 75% or above on both sections. The cost of scheduling too early is $35 per retake plus the 30-day wait if you hit three failures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

How is studying for the Virginia insurance exam different from studying for a state that requires a prelicensing course?

In states that mandate prelicensing hours, the course provides a structured curriculum, a defined pace, a completion requirement, and often a proctored practice exam before the real test. Virginia's no-requirement policy means you must supply all of that structure yourself. The content you need to know is exactly the same — the exam tests the same material whether or not you took a formal course. The difference is accountability. Self-studying candidates who treat preparation casually tend to underestimate the state law section, overestimate their national content readiness, and schedule the exam before they are genuinely prepared. The antidote is building the structure that a mandatory course would have provided: a timeline, a content checklist, timed practice exams, and a clear readiness benchmark before scheduling.

What free resources are available for Virginia insurance exam prep?

Prometric offers free practice exams at prometric.com/virginia/insurance — these are not scored as pass/fail and do not mirror the exact Virginia exam, but they familiarize you with the question format and computer interface. The Prometric content outline for your specific series is available for free download and is the most authoritative guide to what the exam covers. Virginia's Title 38.2 statutes are publicly available at law.lis.virginia.gov and are the source for all state law exam questions. The SCC Bureau of Insurance's website (scc.virginia.gov/pages/Bureau-of-Insurance) has producer licensing information relevant to the licensing and regulation section of the state law exam. These free resources are valuable supplements — but candidates who rely exclusively on free materials typically score lower than those who use structured practice question banks with detailed answer explanations.

Should I take the Series 11-01 combined exam or the separate Life/Annuities and Health exams?

The combined Series 11-01 exam is typically the better choice for candidates who intend to sell both life and health products, because it covers all three lines in a single sitting and allows you to apply for Life, Annuities, and Health authority in one application. Taking separate exams (Series 11-05 for Life & Annuities and Series 11-06 for Health) means two separate registration fees ($70 total instead of $35), two separate test day appointments, and two separate preparation tracks — though each individual exam is shorter (90 scored questions vs. 140). The combined exam's advantage is efficiency. The separate exam approach makes sense only if you know with certainty that you want only one line initially and may add the other later, or if you want to maximize focus on a single line's content at a time.

What is the hardest section of the Virginia insurance exam for most candidates?

For the L/A/H exam (Series 11-01), the Virginia state law section consistently trips up candidates who under-prepare it — particularly the health insurance statutes, Medicare supplement regulations, and long-term care provisions. The national health insurance content (managed care, HMOs, PPOs, coordination of benefits) is also considered the most conceptually complex section of the national portion. For the P&C exam (Series 11-03), the casualty section of the national content — particularly liability insurance concepts, commercial lines structures, and the BOP — tends to be harder than property content for candidates without industry background. The Virginia auto insurance section of the state law portion is heavily tested and reflects three years of significant law changes (2023 UIM stacking, 2024 mandatory insurance, 2025 minimum coverage increases) — candidates who have not specifically studied these updates are likely to miss multiple questions.

How long before my exam should I stop studying new material?

Two days before the exam, stop introducing new topics. Your brain consolidates information during rest and sleep, and cramming new material in the final 48 hours often creates confusion rather than clarity by mixing half-learned new content with well-established prior learning. Spend the final two days doing light review of your weakest areas using flashcards or condensed notes — not absorbing new sections. The evening before the exam, review only material you already know well (to reinforce confidence, not to learn something new), prepare your ID and logistics, and go to sleep at a reasonable hour. A rested brain retrieves known information more reliably than an exhausted one, regardless of how many additional hours were spent studying.

The Virginia insurance exam rewards structured preparation. Build the plan, execute it systematically, measure your readiness honestly, and schedule the exam when your practice scores demonstrate you are ready — not when your calendar says you should be.
