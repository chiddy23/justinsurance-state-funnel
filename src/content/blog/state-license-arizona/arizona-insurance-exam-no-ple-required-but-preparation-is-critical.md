Arizona's no-[prelicense](/arizona/prelicensing)-education approach gives candidates flexibility in how they prepare — but it doesn't reduce what they need to know. A single 70%-correct passing standard, a strict four-attempt annual limit per line, and a body of Arizona-specific content drawn straight from A.R.S. Title 20 all mean that "no PLE required" doesn't mean "no preparation needed." Understanding what Arizona's exam actually covers, and building a study approach that reaches both national insurance principles and Arizona's own statutes, is essential.

Here's everything candidates need to know about Arizona's exam approach.

## Arizona Does Not Require PLE — Context and Implications

Arizona joined the growing group of states without mandatory prelicense education. Unlike Michigan (which maintains a 20-hour per-line PLE requirement), Arizona allows candidates to proceed directly from the decision to get licensed to scheduling the state exam.

States without PLE (context): Pennsylvania (April 2025), North Carolina (October 2025), Massachusetts, South Carolina, Alabama, Nevada, Arizona, and others have all eliminated or never required PLE. The trend reflects regulatory recognition that exam-only licensing is sufficient when quality study materials are available.

Arizona's specific reasoning: Arizona's insurance regulatory philosophy favors market accessibility, particularly given the state's substantial population growth and demand for insurance professionals.

What this means practically:

No minimum study hours required

No formal course completion certificate needed before exam

No waiting period between deciding to get licensed and scheduling the exam

Complete flexibility in how you prepare

What this does NOT mean:

The exam is easier or less comprehensive

Arizona-specific content is less important

Preparation is optional (given the 4-attempt annual limit)

## How Arizona Scoring Actually Works

Arizona scores your exam once. The PSI Arizona Insurance Candidate Information Bulletin states the standard in a single line: "You must get 70% correct to pass the examination." Your score appears on screen at the end of the exam and a score report is emailed to you.

It's worth being direct about a claim that circulates widely in Arizona exam-prep material: that Arizona splits the exam into a "General Knowledge" section and an "Arizona State Law" section that must each independently clear 70%, with no averaging between them. That is not what Arizona's official materials say. Neither phrase appears anywhere in the PSI bulletin, and DIFI's published content outlines are single integrated outlines whose topic weights sum to 100% — not two separately scored halves. Don't build a study strategy around a rule the state does not publish.

What is true is subtler, and just as consequential for how you prepare: Arizona statutory content is threaded through the whole outline rather than quarantined in one block. The bulletin tells candidates directly that the outlines contain "a section relating to Arizona Revised Statutes ('ARS') and Arizona Administrative Code ('AAC')" and recommends studying A.R.S. Title 20 and A.A.C. Title 20, Chapter 6 alongside general material.

Because there is one combined score, weak Arizona-law performance won't fail you by itself the way a separate pass/fail section would — but every state-law question you miss comes straight out of the same 70% you need overall. Miss enough of them and no amount of general insurance knowledge makes the arithmetic work.

What this means for preparation:

Arizona state law is not a minor appendix to skim — those questions are scored exactly like every other question

Time spent on A.R.S. Title 20 is how you protect your overall percentage

Generic national study materials that cover only national content leave the Arizona questions to chance

Score report after failure: if you fail, PSI emails a diagnostic report showing your strengths and weaknesses by content area, and you can view your score reports through your PSI online account. That breakdown is what tells you where to focus for a retake.

## The Four-Attempt Annual Limit

Arizona's exam retake limit (A.R.S. § 20-284) is among the strictest nationally:

Maximum 4 attempts per line of authority within a 12-month period

After the 4th failed attempt: must wait one full year from the date of that last attempt before retesting

Every attempt costs another exam fee — at Arizona's current PSI fees, four failed attempts runs $168 to $236 depending on the line

Why this limit matters: Unlike states with unlimited retakes (Michigan) or no cooling-off periods, Arizona's limit means candidates who approach the exam underprepared risk locking themselves out for a full year. This makes adequate preparation before each attempt — particularly the first — significantly more valuable.

PSI scheduling after failing: you cannot book a new appointment on the same day you tested, because results still have to process. PSI's Arizona bulletin gives the example that a candidate who tests unsuccessfully on a Wednesday can call Thursday and retest as soon as Friday, subject to seat availability — so a retake is typically one to two days out, not the same afternoon.

Strategic implication: Take the exam when you're genuinely ready. Don't rush to schedule after two weeks of minimal study — the 4-attempt limit means each attempt matters.

## Arizona Exam Specifications

Current exam administrator: PSI Services (replaced the previous vendor effective September 3, 2025).

PSI website: test-takers.psiexams.com/anzins

Exam fees: set per line, not flat. Per the current PSI bulletin fee table — Life (Series 13-31) $50, Accident and Health or Sickness (13-32) $50, Life, Accident and Health or Sickness (13-33) $59, Property (13-42) $50, Casualty (13-43) $42, Property and Casualty (13-34) $59, Personal Lines (13-44) $50. Examination fees are not refundable.

Single ID required: One valid government-issued photo ID with signature.

Results: Displayed on screen immediately after completing the exam, with a score report emailed to you and available in your PSI online account.

## Building Your Arizona Study Approach

Step 1: Choose a quality Arizona-specific prep course. Even without PLE requirements, Arizona-specific content requires dedicated study. Courses specifically addressing A.R.S. Title 20, DIFI regulations, and Arizona market-specific provisions produce better state section outcomes.

Step 2: Allocate study time proportionally. Every question counts the same toward your 70%. If you have a strong insurance background, weight Arizona state law heavily in your preparation — that's where your unearned points are hiding. If you're new to insurance, allocate proportional time to both.

Step 3: Practice Arizona-specific questions specifically. Generic national practice question banks don't adequately cover A.R.S. Title 20 content. Seek [practice questions](/arizona/practice-exam) specifically addressing DIFI authority, Arizona policy provisions, and Arizona Guaranty Association limits.

Step 4: Take full-length practice exams under simulated conditions. 2 hours (single line) or 2.5 hours (combined) under timed conditions.

Step 5: Target 80%+ on practice exams before scheduling. The 10-point buffer above 70% protects against exam day variance — particularly important given the 4-attempt annual limit.

Step 6: Don't schedule until ready. Given the 4-attempt annual limit, waiting one or two additional weeks to reach 80%+ practice exam confidence is always worth it.

## Common Arizona Exam Failures

Underestimating the state content. The most common Arizona exam failure pattern: strong general knowledge, weak Arizona-specific performance. Those questions are scored right alongside everything else, so missing them is the fastest way to fall under 70%.

Using only national study materials. Generic national materials don't cover A.R.S. Title 20, DIFI authority, Arizona Guaranty Association limits ($300K life, $250K annuity, $500K health), or Arizona's free-look and right-to-return rules adequately.

Scheduling too quickly without reaching practice confidence. The 4-attempt annual limit means premature scheduling carries real consequences.

Ignoring Arizona's unique CE structure. Arizona's 48-hour/4-year CE cycle appears on exam questions — know this is different from most states' 24-hour/2-year frameworks.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- Why doesn't Arizona require PLE? Arizona's regulatory philosophy favors market accessibility. The state has never required PLE for insurance producer licensure, reflecting confidence that exam-based competency assessment plus ongoing CE requirements are sufficient to maintain professional standards.
- What's the most important thing to know about Arizona's passing score? It's a single standard: PSI's Arizona bulletin says "You must get 70% correct to pass the examination." Arizona does not publish a rule requiring you to clear 70% separately on a "General Knowledge" and an "Arizona State Law" section — that claim appears in third-party prep material, not in the bulletin or DIFI's content outlines. Practically, though, Arizona-law questions count toward the same 70%, so skipping them is still how candidates fail.
- What are the most tested Arizona-specific topics? DIFI authority and licensing requirements, Arizona unfair trade practices, Arizona Guaranty Association limits ($300K life, $250K annuity, $500K health), Arizona's free-look and right-to-return periods (annuities: 10 days, or 30 days at age 65+ under A.R.S. § 20-1233; individual disability and health: 10 days under A.A.C. R20-6-501), replacement regulations, annuity suitability, and the 4-year/48-hour CE framework.
- What happens after my fourth failed attempt in a year? You must wait one full year from the date of your last attempt before retesting on that line of authority (A.R.S. § 20-284(H)). Fees run per line — $42 to $59 per attempt — and are not refundable, so four failed attempts costs roughly $168 to $236.
- Can I take the Arizona exam online? Yes. Arizona offers remote proctored testing through PSI Bridge. Check system compatibility at syscheck.bridge.psiexams.com before scheduling. Remote testing requires a compatible computer, webcam, and quiet private environment.
