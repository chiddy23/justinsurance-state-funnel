North Carolina auto insurance has some of the most distinctive legal features in the country — including mandatory uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, pure contributory negligence (one of only a handful of states still using this standard), the Safe Driver Incentive Plan, and significant new minimum coverage requirements that took effect July 1, 2025. For producers serving North Carolina personal lines clients, understanding these distinctive auto insurance laws is essential both for compliance and for helping clients make genuinely informed coverage decisions.

Here's what every North Carolina agent must know about NC auto insurance laws.

## North Carolina's Fault System

North Carolina is an at-fault (tort) state — the driver who causes an accident is responsible for the other party's damages. This is the baseline for understanding all NC auto insurance.

Contrast with no-fault states: In no-fault states (like Michigan, Florida, and New York), each driver's own insurance pays their medical costs regardless of who caused the accident. North Carolina doesn't use this system — fault matters significantly.

Pure Contributory Negligence (distinctive):

North Carolina applies pure contributory negligence — one of the strictest fault standards in the country used in only a small number of states. Under this doctrine:

If you are even 1% at fault for an accident, you cannot recover any damages from the other party.

This is dramatically different from most states that use comparative negligence systems where your damages are reduced proportionally to your fault. In North Carolina, 1% fault = zero recovery.

Example: A driver runs a red light and hits you, but a court finds you were 1% at fault for not seeing the car coming. Under pure contributory negligence, you recover nothing from the at-fault driver. Your own medical coverage (if you have it) or your own UM/UIM coverage becomes your only source of recovery.

Why this matters for producers:

Clients who think "I wasn't at fault" may be surprised by contested fault findings

Adequate UM/UIM limits become even more important in a contributory negligence state

Higher liability limits protect clients since fault can be contested

## The Last Clear Chance Doctrine

North Carolina recognizes the Last Clear Chance Doctrine as an exception to pure contributory negligence:

How it works: Even if the plaintiff was negligent, if the defendant had the last clear opportunity to avoid the accident and failed to take it, the plaintiff can still recover damages.

Elements plaintiff must show:

Plaintiff was in a position of immediate peril

Plaintiff was unable to remove themselves from danger

Defendant knew of the danger

Defendant had a clear opportunity to act

Defendant failed to act

Plaintiff was harmed as a result

The Last Clear Chance Doctrine is regularly tested on North Carolina Property and Casualty exam questions.

## July 1, 2025 Minimum Coverage Increase

A major recent change: North Carolina's minimum auto insurance requirements increased effective July 1, 2025. This was the first significant minimum increase in many years.

New minimums (effective July 1, 2025):

Bodily Injury Liability:

$50,000 per person (was $30,000)

$100,000 per accident (was $60,000)

Property Damage Liability:

$50,000 per accident (was $25,000)

Commonly written as 50/100/50.

Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist (UM/UIM):

Mandatory at the same minimums as liability

$50,000 per person / $100,000 per accident / $50,000 property damage

Cannot be waived in North Carolina

## Mandatory Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist Coverage

UM/UIM is mandatory in North Carolina — it cannot be waived. This distinguishes NC from states where UM/UIM is optional.

What UM/UIM covers:

Uninsured Motorist (UM): Protects you when the at-fault driver has no insurance

Underinsured Motorist (UIM): Protects you when the at-fault driver has insufficient coverage

Required minimums: Same as liability (50/100/50 after July 1, 2025).

Combined with pure contributory negligence: NC's mandatory UM/UIM is especially important because fault can be contested. If you're involved in an accident and fault is disputed, your own UM/UIM coverage may be your primary source of recovery.

UM/UIM stacking: Available for multi-vehicle policies — important consideration for households with multiple vehicles.

## Safe Driver Incentive Plan (SDIP)

North Carolina's Safe Driver Incentive Plan (SDIP) is a points-based system affecting premium rates based on driving record:

How SDIP works:

Insurance points assigned for at-fault accidents and moving violations

Points directly increase premiums for affected policies

Points remain on record for a specified surcharge period

July 1, 2025 SDIP changes:

Surcharge period for insurance points and speeding convictions (10 mph or less over limit): Increased from 3 years to 5 years

Inexperienced operator surcharge: Increased from 3 years to 8 years for newly insured motorists

Why SDIP matters for producers:

Clients with recent violations or at-fault accidents face substantial premium increases

Understanding SDIP helps explain premium changes to clients

High-risk drivers may need specialty market options

New drivers face 8-year inexperienced operator surcharges — important for families with teen drivers

## Electronic Insurance Monitoring

North Carolina uses electronic insurance verification through the NC Division of Motor Vehicles (NCDMV):

How it works:

NCDMV receives electronic notices when insurance policies lapse

Lapse triggers notification process

Driving without required insurance can result in:

License revocation

Registration revocation

Substantial restoration fees

Misdemeanor charges for driving on revoked license

Producer implication: Clients who let policies lapse — even briefly — face significant reinstatement costs and license issues. Emphasizing continuous coverage is both good advice and E&O protection.

## What's Required vs. Optional

Required coverage (effective July 1, 2025):

Bodily Injury Liability: 50/100/50

Property Damage Liability: 50/100/50

Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist: 50/100/50 (mandatory, cannot be waived)

Optional but recommended:

MedPay (Medical Payments) — covers your own medical expenses regardless of fault (no PIP required in NC)

Collision — covers your own vehicle damage in at-fault accidents

Comprehensive — covers non-collision losses (theft, weather, animal strikes, vandalism)

Rental reimbursement

Roadside assistance

Lender requirements:

Financed vehicles typically require collision and comprehensive

These aren't required by NC law but are contractual obligations to lenders

## Pure Contributory Negligence: Producer Implications

Given pure contributory negligence, adequate UM/UIM and liability limits take on special importance in NC:

Higher liability limits protect you: If you cause an accident and your limits are exhausted, you're personally liable for the excess. North Carolina's $50,000/$100,000 minimums may be inadequate for serious accidents.

UM/UIM limits protect you: When the at-fault driver has insufficient coverage — or when fault is contested — your UM/UIM coverage may be your only recovery.

Minimum coverage is the floor, not the recommendation:

$50,000 per person might not cover serious injuries

$50,000 property damage may not cover modern vehicle values

Many professionals recommend 100/300/100 or higher

NC Rate Bureau forms: As noted in the exam content, North Carolina auto policies use NC Rate Bureau forms rather than ISO Personal Auto Policy forms. Rate Bureau forms have NC-specific provisions affecting how coverage works.

## Additional Legal Provisions

Statute of limitations: 3 years to file a personal injury claim after an accident.

Punitive damages: Capped at $250,000 or three times compensatory damages (whichever is greater). Available when defendant acted fraudulently, maliciously, or wantonly.

One-party consent for recording: North Carolina is a one-party consent state for audio recording — relevant for dashcam and similar recording considerations.

Proof of financial responsibility: Required at registration renewal, registration, and vehicle inspections.

Texting laws: Illegal to use a mobile phone while operating a vehicle in North Carolina.

## Rate Bureau Forms and NC-Specific Provisions

North Carolina auto insurance uses NC Rate Bureau Personal Auto Policy forms rather than standard ISO forms:

NC-specific provisions:

Policy language developed by the NC Rate Bureau

Specific to NC regulatory requirements

Distinctive from standard ISO PAP

Exam questions specifically reference NC Rate Bureau personal auto provisions

Producers working with auto clients should be familiar with the NC Rate Bureau form's specific provisions rather than relying entirely on ISO PAP knowledge.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- What are North Carolina's new auto insurance minimums? Effective July 1, 2025, North Carolina requires 50/100/50 — $50,000 bodily injury per person, $100,000 per accident, and $50,000 property damage liability. UM/UIM coverage is mandatory at the same minimums.
- What is pure contributory negligence in North Carolina? Pure contributory negligence means that if you're even 1% at fault for an accident, you cannot recover any damages from the other party. North Carolina is one of only a few states still using this strict standard.
- Can I waive UM/UIM coverage in North Carolina? No. Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage is mandatory in North Carolina and cannot be waived. It must be carried at the same minimums as liability coverage (50/100/50).
- What is the Safe Driver Incentive Plan? The SDIP is North Carolina's points-based system that assigns insurance points for at-fault accidents and moving violations, directly increasing premiums. As of July 1, 2025, surcharge periods increased to 5 years for violations and 8 years for newly insured/inexperienced operators.
- Does North Carolina require PIP (Personal Injury Protection)? No. North Carolina is an at-fault state, not a no-fault state, and does not require PIP. Medical Payments (MedPay) coverage is available as an optional add-on covering your own medical expenses regardless of fault.
