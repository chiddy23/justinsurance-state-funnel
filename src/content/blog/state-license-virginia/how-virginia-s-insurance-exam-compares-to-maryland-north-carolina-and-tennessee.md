Virginia sits at the crossroads of the Mid-Atlantic and Southeast, and producers who want to serve the full DC-to-Charlotte corridor — or the Appalachian corridor into Tennessee — regularly pursue multi-state licensing across all four states. Virginia, Maryland, North Carolina, and Tennessee split into two exam-vendor pairs, share the same no-mandatory-[prelicensing](/virginia/prelicensing) status today, but still differ meaningfully on fee schedules, fingerprinting, and renewal timelines. For candidates planning their initial licensing strategy and for producers building multi-state authority through reciprocity, understanding the differences avoids costly surprises.

## Exam Vendor

Only two vendors serve all four states. Virginia and Maryland both use Prometric; North Carolina and Tennessee both use Pearson VUE. Candidates building VA+MD authority or NC+TN authority navigate one vendor instead of two, though each state still runs its own scheduling site, check-in procedure, and ID requirements.

## Prelicensing Education Requirements

Virginia dropped its prelicensing mandate long before the rest of the region did. Virginia repealed mandatory prelicensing education for its major lines under Acts 2001, c. 706, effective September 1, 2002 (former Va. Code § 38.2-1816); title insurance is the one exception, and it still carries a separate 16-hour course requirement under Va. Code § 38.2-1814.1. Maryland, North Carolina, and Tennessee each required prelicensing hours until recently — 20 hours per single line in all three, with Tennessee's 40-hour figure applying to a combined-lines license rather than to a single line — but all three have since repealed those mandates: Tennessee in 2023, Maryland in 2024, and North Carolina in 2025. A candidate planning to get licensed in all four states can go straight to the exam in every one of them; structured prep courses remain available in all four states and are strongly recommended given the state-law content, but none of them is required.

For a dual-line (L/A/H + P&C) candidate pursuing all four states, none currently requires completing prelicensing hours before scheduling an exam — the meaningful differences between the states now are exam vendor, fees, fingerprinting, and CE, not prelicensing.

Time-to-exam is now similar across the region: none of the four states adds mandatory coursework before you can schedule. Remaining timeline differences come down to processing speed, fingerprinting logistics, and whether a state requires the application to be submitted before the exam (North Carolina does; the other three do not).

## Exam Structure and Fees

Virginia's $35 exam fee is the lowest of the four states. North Carolina ($45) is close. Tennessee ($55) and Maryland ($62) are progressively higher. For a dual-line candidate taking all four states' exams, Virginia offers the cheapest path while delivering the same licensing credential.

All four states build their exams on the same national content framework, so the core insurance product knowledge is largely identical — but they do not report scores the same way, and that difference matters when you compare them. Virginia and Maryland use Prometric and report a raw percentage: your total score is the number of questions you answered correctly divided by the total number of questions, and 70% passes. North Carolina and Tennessee use Pearson VUE and report a **scaled** score on a 0–100 range with a passing score of 70. As both Pearson VUE handbooks state, the scaled score "is neither the number of questions you answered correctly nor the percentage of questions you answered correctly" — raw scores are statistically equated across exam forms first, so the number of correct answers needed varies by the version you happen to sit. Treat 70 in North Carolina and Tennessee as a reported cut score, not as "70% correct."

## Score Validity Windows

Score validity is set by each state's insurance department, not by the testing vendor. Virginia's window is 183 calendar days, and the statute is stricter than the shorthand suggests. Va. Code § 38.2-1817(F) provides that if an applicant "fails to obtain the appropriate license from the Commission within 183 calendar days from the date he passes the examination, the examination grade shall be considered invalid and the examination fee and application processing fee shall be forfeited." What has to happen inside the window is *issuance*, not filing — the Bureau's review time counts against you, not against the clock. So a Virginia candidate does not really have six months of slack; subtract the Bureau's processing (roughly 15 business days on a clean file, 30 to 60 days where there are convictions to review) and treat the practical deadline as several weeks earlier than day 183. Confirm the corresponding window with the Maryland Insurance Administration, NCDOI, or the Tennessee Department of Commerce and Insurance before you sequence exams across states — those three set their own periods, and the vendor cannot change them.

## Fingerprinting and Background Checks

Fingerprinting requirements differ across all four states, and not all of them fingerprint at all. Virginia routes producer fingerprinting through Fieldprint. Maryland does not require fingerprinting — the state relies on a non-fingerprint, name-based background check instead. North Carolina fingerprints through local police departments rather than a commercial vendor. Tennessee uses IdentoGO. No two of the four states share the same process, so confirm the correct vendor (or lack of one) before you schedule.

## Application Fees

Virginia's per-line fee structure is the most granular — $15 per qualification versus a single flat application fee in the other states. That makes Virginia the cheapest in every scenario, not just for single-line applicants. A single-qualification applicant pays $15. Even a producer adding all the major qualifications — Life & Annuities, Health, and Property & Casualty (a single combined line in Virginia) — pays just $45, still below Maryland's $54 flat fee, Tennessee's $50, and North Carolina's $82. There is no realistic line count at which Virginia's per-qualification fees add up to more than the other states' flat application fees.

## CE Requirements at a Glance

Virginia's 16-hour single-license CE requirement is the lowest of the four states. For dual-line producers, all four states require 24 hours — but Virginia's 16/24-hour differential based on license count is unique to this state. Virginia's birth-month renewal deadline means every producer's individual renewal date repeats every two years, which is simple to track once you know your own date, even though it isn't a single shared deadline for everyone.

## Reciprocity Across All Four States

All four states participate in NAIC reciprocal licensing. Once you hold a resident license in any one of them, you can obtain non-resident authority in the others through NIPR or Sircon without retaking any exam. The application fees are: Maryland ($54 flat), North Carolina ($82 flat), Tennessee ($50 flat), and Virginia ($15 per line).

Virginia-based producers expanding into Maryland, North Carolina, or Tennessee through reciprocity pay each state's own non-resident application fee rather than Virginia's per-line structure — no exam is required for any of the three.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Which of these four states is the fastest path to a first insurance license?

Virginia — by a narrow margin over Maryland and Tennessee, and by a wide one over North Carolina — because none of the four states requires prelicensing education anymore. A Virginia candidate can schedule the Prometric exam immediately after deciding to get licensed and complete the entire process in two to four weeks. Maryland and Tennessee have also repealed their prelicensing mandates (Tennessee in 2023, Maryland in 2024), so candidates there go straight to their exam vendor and land in a similar 2-4 week window. North Carolina repealed its mandate in 2025 too, but it remains the slowest of the four on the back end: NCDOI's official processing standard is 60 days, and a realistic end-to-end timeline there is 4-8 weeks rather than 2-4. For a new producer in the DC-Maryland-Virginia area who wants to start working as quickly as possible, getting Virginia-licensed first and then adding Maryland non-resident authority through NIPR is typically the fastest combined path — helped along by Maryland's lack of a fingerprinting requirement.

Did Tennessee ever require more prelicensing hours than Maryland and North Carolina?

Only for combined-lines candidates. Tennessee's requirement was 20 hours for a single line — the same as Maryland's and North Carolina's — and 40 hours for a combined-lines license. The 40-hour figure is often quoted as though it were Tennessee's per-line number; it was not. All three states have since eliminated their prelicensing mandates entirely: Tennessee repealed its requirement first, in 2023, followed by Maryland in 2024 and North Carolina in 2025. Today none of the three requires any prelicensing hours, and candidates in all three can go straight to their exam vendor (Pearson VUE for Tennessee and North Carolina, Prometric for Maryland). Tennessee's exam fee ($55) is lower than Maryland's ($62) but higher than North Carolina's ($45). For Virginia producers expanding into Tennessee, non-resident licensing through NIPR requires no prelicensing and no exam.

If I hold a Virginia license and want to add Maryland non-resident authority, how long does the Maryland application take?

Non-resident applications in Maryland processed through NIPR typically take 7-10 business days — materially faster than Virginia's own 15-20 business days (30-60 where an applicant answers yes to a background question). Maryland being the quicker of the two is exactly why the Virginia-first, Maryland-second sequence works. The process involves submitting a non-resident application through NIPR ($54 application fee), confirming your Virginia resident license is in good standing (NIPR verifies this automatically through the NAIC database), and waiting for Maryland's Insurance Administration to process the application. No exam, no fingerprinting, and no CHRR is required for the Maryland non-resident application. Once approved, you hold both a Virginia resident license and a Maryland non-resident license, allowing you to sell insurance to clients in both states without restriction.

Do CE courses completed in Virginia count toward CE requirements in Maryland, North Carolina, or Tennessee?

For non-resident producers, generally yes — each state accepts CE compliance from the producer's home state as satisfying the non-resident CE requirement. A Virginia-resident producer who is CE-compliant in Virginia (16 hours for single license, 24 for dual) is considered CE-compliant for their non-resident licenses in Maryland, North Carolina, and Tennessee. However, specialty line training requirements — particularly LTC Partnership training, annuity best interest training, and NFIP flood training — may require state-specific components regardless of non-resident status. Always confirm specialty line requirements with each state before selling those products.

Do I have to pass the national and state law sections separately in any of these states?

No — and it is worth being precise about this, because the opposite is widely repeated. Virginia scores the exam as a single total: the Bureau of Insurance bulletin states that your total percentage score is computed by dividing the number of questions you answered correctly by the total number of questions on the examination, and that the per-section percentages on your score report are provided to guide your review, not as separate hurdles. North Carolina is explicit in the same direction — its two-part exams are "scored together, not individually," and candidates must achieve an overall passing score rather than a passing score for each part. Maryland and Tennessee likewise report one overall result. The practical implication is the reverse of the folklore: a very strong national section *can* offset a marginal state law section in all four states. That is not a reason to underprepare on state law, which carries roughly a quarter of the questions on most of these exams and is where unprepared candidates lose the most ground — but you are not facing a second, independent cut score.

Virginia, Maryland, North Carolina, and Tennessee together cover the full corridor from the Potomac to the Smokies. Understanding how each state's process differs — and leveraging Virginia's low exam fee and per-line application structure as a starting point — gives producers the most efficient path to multi-state authority across the region.
