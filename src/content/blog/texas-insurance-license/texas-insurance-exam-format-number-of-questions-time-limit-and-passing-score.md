Knowing what you're walking into on exam day removes a lot of unnecessary anxiety. The Texas insurance exam has a specific format — and understanding it helps you pace yourself, manage your time, and show up prepared.

Here's exactly what to expect.

## Who Administers the Exam?

The Texas Department of Insurance [contracts with Pearson VUE to administer all insurance licensing exams in Texas](https://nipr.com/licensing-center/state-information/texas). You'll take the exam at a Pearson VUE testing center. Appointments can be scheduled online at pearsonvue.com.

## Exam Breakdown by License Type

Texas builds each producer exam out of two content outlines: a general knowledge section and a Texas state-specific section. Both are delivered as one sitting under one clock. The question counts below come from the [Texas insurance content outlines effective December 1, 2025](https://www.pearsonvue.com/content/dam/VUE/vue/en/documents/publications/124400.pdf); the time limits come from Pearson VUE's Texas insurance licensing candidate handbook.

-   **General Lines — Life, Accident & Health:** 130 scored questions (100 general knowledge + 30 Texas-specific), plus 15 unscored pretest questions, for 145 items on screen. **150 minutes.**
-   **Life Agent:** 80 scored questions (50 general knowledge + 30 Texas-specific), plus 10 unscored pretest questions, for 90 items on screen. **120 minutes.**
-   **General Lines — Property & Casualty:** 130 scored questions (100 general knowledge + 30 Texas-specific), plus 15 unscored pretest questions, for 145 items on screen. **150 minutes.**
-   **Personal Lines Property & Casualty:** 100 scored questions (75 general knowledge + 25 Texas-specific), plus 10 unscored pretest questions, for 110 items on screen. **120 minutes.**

Most candidates on the life and health track sit for the General Lines — Life, Accident & Health exam, which is what Texas issues instead of separate life-only and health-only agent licenses. The narrower Life Agent license has its own, shorter exam.

Do the arithmetic on the pacing before test day. On the Life, Accident & Health exam that's 145 questions in 150 minutes — a hair over a minute each, with nothing left over for a leisurely second pass. On the Life Agent exam it's 90 questions in 120 minutes, which is closer to 80 seconds apiece.

## What Types of Questions Are on the Exam?

All questions are multiple choice with four answer options. There's no partial credit — each question is either right or wrong. The exam pulls from two main content areas:

General insurance knowledge — concepts, policy types, underwriting, claims

Texas state law — TDI regulations, agent duties, replacement rules, consumer protections

The Texas-specific share is bigger than most candidates assume, and it isn't the same on every exam. On the Life, Accident & Health exam, Texas law is 30 of the 130 scored questions — about 23%. On the Life Agent exam it's 30 of 80, or roughly 38%, because the general knowledge half is smaller. Either way, the state section is where state-specific study material earns its keep, and it's graded on the same scale as everything else.

## What to Expect on Exam Day

Report to the test center [at least 30 minutes before your exam time](https://www.pearsonvue.com/content/dam/VUE/vue/en/documents/publications/124400.pdf) — Pearson VUE's Texas handbook says candidates "should report to the test center at least thirty (30) minutes before the examination begins to complete registration," and it repeats the 30-minute figure in its exam-day hints. Showing up at the 15-minute mark is how people lose a non-refundable exam fee.

Bring [two forms of ID, one of which must be a government-issued photo ID](https://www.pearsonvue.com/content/dam/VUE/vue/en/documents/publications/124400.pdf). Pearson VUE does not recognize ID grace periods — an expired license is expired.

Leave everything else in the car or a locker. No personal items are allowed in the testing room, and the handbook's list specifically includes phones, watches, wallets, bags, books, notes, and your own pens or pencils.

Don't bring paper. Dictionaries, books, papers (including scratch paper), and reference materials are barred from the exam room. Once you're seated, the test administrator provides the materials you'll use to make notes or calculations — you may not write on them before the exam starts, and you may not take them out of the room.

Results are displayed immediately after you finish, and you leave with an official score report in hand.

## How Is the Score Calculated?

Your score is based only on the scored questions. The handbook is direct about the rest: pretest questions are being trialled for future exams, "responses to pretest questions do not affect a candidate's score," and they are "mixed in with the scored questions and are not identified." The number of them is published in the heading of each content outline — 15 on the Life, Accident & Health exam, 10 on the Life Agent exam — but you will never know which fifteen they were. Answer every question as if it counts, because most of them do. There's no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave one blank.

Here's the part almost everyone gets wrong: **Texas reports a scaled score, not a percentage.** You need a 70 to pass, and that 70 is [a published cut score set by the Texas Department of Insurance with Pearson VUE](https://www.pearsonvue.com/content/dam/VUE/vue/en/documents/publications/124400.pdf) — but it is not "70% of the questions." Because different forms of the exam vary slightly in difficulty, raw scores are equated and converted onto a common 0–100 reporting scale. In the handbook's own words: "The scaled score that is reported to you is neither the number of questions you answered correctly nor the percentage of questions you answered correctly. With a passing score of 70, any score below 70 indicates how close the candidate came to passing, rather than the actual number or percentage of questions the candidates answered correctly."

Practically, that means a 68 on your score report doesn't tell you that you missed 32% of the questions — it tells you how far short of the standard you landed. Don't try to reverse-engineer a question count from it.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- Can I skip questions and come back to them? Yes. Pearson VUE's testing platform allows you to flag questions and return to them before submitting. Use this to your advantage — answer what you know first, then revisit the harder ones.
- What happens if I run out of time? The exam will auto-submit when time expires. Unanswered questions are counted as incorrect, so always make your best guess rather than leaving anything blank.
- Do I get my score right away? Yes. Your pass or fail result appears on screen immediately after you complete the exam. A score report is also printed and given to you at the testing center.
- Is the exam the same every time? The questions are drawn from a large question bank, so no two exams are identical. The topics covered remain consistent, but the specific questions will vary.
- Can I use a calculator during the exam? Yes, and you need to bring your own — the Texas handbook states that "[Calculators are NOT provided by the test center staff](https://www.pearsonvue.com/content/dam/VUE/vue/en/documents/publications/124400.pdf)." Yours is permitted only if it is silent, hand-held, nonprinting, and without an alphabetic keypad; financial calculators are not allowed, and a malfunction is not grounds for extra time or a challenge. The math on the exam is straightforward, so a basic calculator is all you need.
