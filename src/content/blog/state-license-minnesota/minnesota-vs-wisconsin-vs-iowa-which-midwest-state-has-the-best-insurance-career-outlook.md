Producers who hold or are considering licenses in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Iowa — three states that share both geography and significant producer overlap along the I-90, I-94, and I-35 corridors — face a genuinely meaningful comparison question. All three states have competitive insurance markets, functioning licensing frameworks, and meaningful employment bases for insurance professionals. But the differences in income benchmarks, market structure, economic drivers, licensing requirements, and career ceiling are real and worth understanding before deciding where to concentrate career development effort. This post provides the complete, data-driven comparison across every dimension that affects the long-term insurance career outlook in each state.

## The Salary Benchmark Comparison

Producer compensation data comes from several sources that measure different populations in different ways, so the first job is establishing what each number actually represents — before comparing states at all.

The most rigorous source is the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics survey. For insurance sales agents in the Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington MSA, BLS reported a mean hourly wage of $50.44 in May 2024 — roughly $104,915 a year. That is a mean across the full distribution, pulled upward by experienced commercial producers and salaried insurance roles, and it is the most useful benchmark for a producer projecting a long-term trajectory in a major metro. Statewide, BLS puts Minnesota insurance agents at an average of $98,220.

Base-salary trackers land considerably lower because they are measuring something else. Salary.com, for example, puts Minnesota's statewide average at $59,527 — a base salary benchmark that excludes the commission and variable income where most producer earnings actually originate. Neither number is wrong. One describes the floor; the other describes the whole package.

What this post will not do is publish a precise state-by-state salary ranking. Producer wage benchmarks for Wisconsin and Iowa shift between survey vintages and between methodologies, and a head-to-head dollar comparison assembled from mismatched sources would imply a precision the underlying data does not support. The defensible comparison is structural rather than numerical — what each state's economy actually generates in insurable premium, because that, not a posted salary range, is what sets a commission-based producer's ceiling. That is the subject of the next section.

## The Economic Foundation: What Drives Insurance Demand in Each State

Insurance income is ultimately a function of the economy being insured — larger premiums generate larger commissions, and more complex accounts generate pricing power for producers who develop relevant expertise. The three states differ meaningfully in the economic drivers that create insurance demand.

### Minnesota

Minnesota's economy is the most complex and highest-value of the three states for insurance purposes. Seventeen Fortune 500 headquarters give the Twin Cities the highest concentration of Fortune-class headquarters per capita in the nation. The healthcare and medical technology sector — Medical Alley — accounts for 16% of national medtech talent and generates professional liability, products liability, and specialty lines demand that neither Wisconsin nor Iowa can match in volume or complexity. Financial services companies including UnitedHealth Group, Ameriprise Financial, and Securian Financial generate D&O, E&O, and cyber liability demand at scale. The Destination Medical Center's 20-year $5.6 billion initiative in Rochester creates a sustained long-term economic growth story. Minnesota's hard property market — 34% average homeowners rate increase in 2025, the highest in the nation — means every renewal generates more commission than it did a year ago.

Minnesota's insurance career ceiling is higher than either Wisconsin or Iowa because the commercial economy supports specialty lines expertise that commands premium-intensive accounts in healthcare liability, medical device products liability, financial institution coverage, and technology E&O that simply do not exist at the same scale in the other two states.

### Wisconsin

Wisconsin's economy is substantial but differently structured. Madison — the state capital and home to the University of Wisconsin system — anchors a government, healthcare, and technology economy. Milwaukee's manufacturing, financial services, and healthcare sectors generate meaningful commercial insurance demand. Wisconsin's agricultural economy — dairy farming concentrated in the western and central parts of the state — creates farm insurance and agricultural products liability opportunity. The manufacturing sector, including companies like Oshkosh Corporation, Harley-Davidson, Johnson Controls, and Rockwell Automation, generates workers' compensation, commercial property, and products liability accounts.

Wisconsin's insurance career ceiling is meaningful but lower than Minnesota's primarily because Wisconsin lacks the Fortune 500 density and the specialty lines complexity of the Twin Cities commercial market. Madison and Milwaukee provide solid commercial lines markets; the rural and mid-market Wisconsin economy provides strong agricultural and manufacturing coverage opportunity.

### Iowa

Iowa's economy is heavily agricultural and manufacturing-oriented, with Des Moines providing a financial services concentration that is disproportionately large for the state's overall size. Principal Financial Group, Nationwide (with major Des Moines operations), EMC Insurance, and Farm Bureau Financial Services are all headquartered or have major operations in Des Moines — making Iowa's capital city a meaningful insurance industry employment hub distinct from its role as a producer market.

Iowa's economy outside Des Moines is predominantly agricultural — corn, soybeans, hogs, poultry, and related processing and supply chain businesses. This creates strong crop insurance, farm property, and agricultural liability opportunity but does not generate the commercial complexity of Minnesota's corporate economy. Iowa's smaller cities — Cedar Rapids, Davenport, Sioux City, Waterloo — have meaningful regional commercial markets but lower average account premiums than comparable Minnesota or Wisconsin cities.

Iowa's insurance career ceiling for field producers is lower than both Minnesota and Wisconsin because the commercial economy generates smaller average accounts. However, Iowa's insurance industry employment concentration — particularly in Des Moines — creates carrier-side career opportunities (underwriting, claims, product development) that compensate for the lower field producer income potential.

## The Licensing Comparison

The three states have meaningfully different licensing requirements that affect both entry costs and reciprocity benefits for producers holding multi-state licenses.

### Minnesota

[Prelicensing](/minnesota/prelicensing): 20 hours per line of authority (40 hours combined Life and A&H). Certification exam required at 70% [pass rate](/pass-rates), proctored by a disinterested third party.

State exam: PSI Services; $45 major lines. Combined P&C: 130 questions/3 hours. Combined Life and A&H: 145 questions/3 hours. Remote testing available via PSI Bridge.

Application fee: $50 per line of authority, plus a single $10 technology surcharge and the $5.60 NIPR transaction fee. Combined P&C — two lines of authority — comes to approximately $115.60 total.

CE: 24 hours biennial; 3 hours ethics; 12 hours non-company-sponsored minimum (satisfiable entirely through self-paced online courses from an independent provider — no live instruction required). No carryover. Renewal fee: $50 per line of authority plus a single $10 technology surcharge — about $60 for a single-line license.

Overall assessment: Minnesota's prelicensing requirement (20 hours per line) is among the more demanding in the region. The investment is higher than Wisconsin or Iowa at entry but produces more comprehensively prepared producers.

### Wisconsin

Prelicensing: Wisconsin requires prelicensing education — exam preparation courses are available and widely used, though the mandatory hour requirement is less strictly defined than Minnesota's 20-hour per-line standard. The prelicensing certificate is valid for one year.

State exam: PSI Services; exam results valid 180 days. Wisconsin's exam validity window (180 days from passing) is significantly shorter than Minnesota's three-year window — a practical difference for candidates who pass the exam and delay application.

Application fee: Competitive with Minnesota.

CE: 24 hours biennial, including a minimum of 3 hours of ethics — the same 24-hour total and the same 3-hour ethics minimum Minnesota requires (Wis. Admin. Code ch. Ins 28; OCI agent CE page). Wisconsin's CE flexibility — all hours, ethics included, can be completed through self-paced online courses — is similar to Minnesota's, where the non-company-sponsored requirement can likewise be satisfied entirely online through an independent provider.

Overall assessment: Wisconsin's licensing requirements are somewhat less demanding at entry than Minnesota's, though the two states' ongoing CE obligations are effectively identical — 24 hours every two years with 3 hours of ethics in both. Both states allow CE to be completed entirely through self-paced online courses. The 180-day exam validity window is the most significant operational difference — candidates must apply promptly after passing.

### Iowa

Prelicensing: Iowa has no mandatory prelicensing education requirement — candidates can sit for the state exam without completing a prescribed course. This is the most permissive prelicensing structure of the three states and represents the lowest barrier to entry.

State exam: Pearson VUE; $44 per exam. Exam results valid 90 days — the shortest validity window of the three states, requiring prompt application after passing.

Application fee: Comparable to other states.

CE: 36 hours triennial (every three years rather than biennial), including 3 hours of ethics. The per-cycle total is larger than Minnesota's or Wisconsin's 24 hours, but the annual pace is identical — 12 CE hours a year in all three states. Iowa also dropped its former 18-hour classroom mandate effective June 9, 2021; all 36 hours may now be completed by classroom, classroom-equivalent, or self-study/online course.

Overall assessment: Iowa is the easiest state to enter (no mandatory prelicensing), and its CE burden is a scheduling difference rather than a heavier one — the same 12 hours a year as Minnesota and Wisconsin, banked over a three-year term instead of a two-year one, with no in-person requirement. The Pearson VUE exam platform differs from the PSI platform used in Minnesota and Wisconsin, requiring platform-specific preparation for candidates testing in all three states.

## The Reciprocity Advantage for Multi-State Producers

All three states have reciprocity agreements with each other and with most NAIC member states, meaning a Minnesota resident producer can obtain Wisconsin and Iowa non-resident licenses without re-taking those states' exams — and vice versa. This creates significant strategic opportunity for border-area producers.

The border opportunity: Producers based near state lines — the Twin Cities near the Wisconsin border, Duluth near Wisconsin, Rochester near Iowa — can hold non-resident licenses in adjacent states and serve clients across state lines. A Minneapolis producer who holds Minnesota resident and Wisconsin non-resident licenses can serve the greater Minneapolis-St. Paul-Eau Claire region without restriction. A Rochester producer who holds Minnesota resident and Iowa non-resident licenses can serve clients in the Rochester-La Crosse-Mason City corridor.

Non-resident CE treatment: All three states exempt non-resident producers from their CE requirements when the producer's home state CE is current. A Minnesota resident producer who completes Minnesota's 24-hour CE requirement automatically satisfies both Wisconsin's and Iowa's non-resident CE obligations without additional course completion. This makes multi-state licensing essentially free of additional compliance burden beyond the initial application fees.

CE structure for dual residents: Wisconsin's CE requirement (24 hours biennial, 3 ethics) does not allow carryover from one renewal period to the next — the same is true of Minnesota. A producer who transitions from Minnesota to Wisconsin residency completes each state's full CE requirement independently, though both states otherwise permit the full CE requirement to be completed through self-paced online courses.

## The Hard Market Context: Property Insurance in All Three States

Minnesota's property insurance hard market — 34% average rate increase in 2025, highest in the nation — is the most severe of the three states but is not isolated. Wisconsin and Iowa have also experienced meaningful property insurance rate increases driven by severe convective weather, though their rate trajectories differ.

Minnesota: Average homeowners premium approximately $3,530 in 2025, projected to rise 4% to $3,654 in 2026. Ranked 9th most expensive nationally (from 21st in 2023). The hard market increases commission on every renewing property account and creates advisory opportunity for producers who help clients navigate nonrenewals, coverage changes, and loss mitigation options.

Wisconsin: Wisconsin has experienced property insurance rate increases but at a less dramatic pace than Minnesota. Wisconsin's severe weather profile — similar hail and windstorm exposure in the southern part of the state — has driven rates upward, but the catastrophic storm events that pushed Minnesota's loss ratios to $1.92 paid per $1.00 in premium in 2022 have not occurred at the same magnitude in Wisconsin.

Iowa: Iowa has also experienced significant homeowners insurance rate increases — the state was among those seeing increases of at least 35% from 2023 to 2025. Iowa's tornado and hail exposure in the southern and western parts of the state creates meaningful convective weather loss experience. The Iowa hard market creates similar advisory opportunity to Minnesota's for producers serving Iowa homeowners.

## The Career Outlook Scorecard

Pulling the analysis together across the dimensions that most affect a producer's long-term outcome:

Income ceiling: Minnesota is the highest of the three, driven by Twin Cities commercial complexity and the specialty lines demand that Fortune 500 density and the Medical Alley medtech cluster generate. Wisconsin is solid through the Madison and Milwaukee commercial markets. Iowa's field-producer ceiling is the lowest of the three — though its Des Moines carrier cluster offers career-side opportunity that field production alone does not.

Entry cost and speed: Iowa is the fastest and least expensive entry, with no mandatory prelicensing education at all. Wisconsin sits in the middle, with prelicensing required and a certificate that expires after a year. Minnesota's 20 hours per line of authority is the largest up-front investment of the three states.

Ongoing compliance: effectively a wash. All three states work out to 12 CE hours a year, none of the three requires classroom instruction, and reciprocity means a producer's home state CE satisfies the other two for non-resident purposes.

Market specialization: Minnesota for healthcare professional liability, medical device products liability, financial institution coverage, and technology E&O. Wisconsin for dairy, agriculture, and mid-market manufacturing. Iowa for crop, farm property, and livestock.

## Making the Choice: Which State Is Right for Your Career

Choose Minnesota if: You want the highest income ceiling, you have or can build professional networks in the Twin Cities commercial economy, you are interested in specialty lines complexity — healthcare professional liability, medical device, financial institution, technology E&O — or you are already in Minnesota and want to maximize the opportunity where you are. Minnesota's combination of Fortune 500 density, hard property market commission tailwind, growing Rochester market, and self-employed population that is systematically underinsured creates more addressable insurance opportunity per square mile of urban commercial territory than either Wisconsin or Iowa.

Choose Wisconsin if: You are in a border area between Minnesota and Wisconsin and want the flexibility of serving both markets, you are focused on agricultural insurance in the dairy belt of central Wisconsin, or you want a commercial lines market that is meaningful but less intensely competitive than the Twin Cities. Wisconsin's Madison and Milwaukee markets provide solid commercial careers; the rural agricultural market creates a different but viable path.

Choose Iowa if: You are focused on agricultural insurance — crop insurance, farm property, livestock — where Iowa's corn, soybean, hog, and specialty crop base creates one of the largest agricultural insurance markets in the country. Iowa's Des Moines insurance industry cluster also creates carrier-side employment opportunities — underwriting, actuarial, claims management, product development — that are not matched by Wisconsin's or Minnesota's insurance industry employment concentration. If your goal is a producer career in agricultural lines, Iowa's combination of enormous crop insurance premium volume, low competition density in rural markets, and deep agricultural community relationships creates a compelling case.

The multi-state strategy: For producers near state borders, the most strategic approach is to hold a resident license in the state where you primarily reside and non-resident licenses in adjacent states — building a client book that reflects geographic reality rather than administrative state lines. A producer based in the Duluth-Superior area naturally serves both Minnesota and Wisconsin clients. A producer in Rochester serves both Minnesota and Iowa clients in the southern Minnesota-northern Iowa corridor. The reciprocity structure makes multi-state licensing cost-effective, and the non-resident CE exemption makes ongoing compliance essentially free beyond initial fees.

## Frequently Asked Questions

I am currently unlicensed and deciding which state to pursue first. Minnesota has higher income potential but harder licensing. Is it worth starting with Iowa or Wisconsin to get licensed faster?

Start where you intend to build your career — not where licensing is easiest. If you plan to work in Minnesota, getting a Minnesota license first is the right decision regardless of the additional prelicensing requirement. The 20-hour prelicensing course is 20 hours — a one-time investment of roughly two to three weeks of evening study. The income differential that Minnesota's commercial economy creates over a 20-year career is substantially larger than the time cost of 20 hours of prelicensing. Starting in Iowa because prelicensing is not required and then trying to transition to Minnesota later means you have built a client book, a referral network, and a carrier appointment structure in a lower-ceiling market that does not transfer directly to Minnesota's commercial environment. Start where you want to finish.

A Wisconsin-based producer asks: should I get a Minnesota non-resident license to access the Twin Cities market?

Yes, with a specific strategy. A Wisconsin resident producer who adds a Minnesota non-resident license gains access to the Twin Cities commercial market and the hard property market without changing their residency. The practical opportunity is serving clients who operate across the Wisconsin-Minnesota border — manufacturers with facilities in both states, agricultural operations near the border, and transportation companies with routes through both states. The Minnesota non-resident license costs the application fee plus $5.60 NIPR transaction fee — a modest investment. Wisconsin's home state CE satisfies Minnesota's non-resident CE requirement, meaning there is no ongoing compliance cost. The only active decision is whether to develop Minnesota-specific carrier appointments and market knowledge, which requires investment in relationships but no additional licensing cost.

How does Iowa's position as an insurance industry employment hub affect its attractiveness for someone considering a career in the industry rather than specifically in field production?

Iowa's Des Moines concentration of insurance carriers — Principal Financial, Nationwide, EMC Insurance, Farm Bureau Financial Services, and others — creates insurance industry career opportunities that Minnesota and Wisconsin cannot match in total volume. For someone whose goal is a career in insurance in roles beyond field production — underwriting, actuarial, claims, product management, compliance, risk management — Des Moines offers a depth of employer options that rivals much larger cities nationally. The field producer income ceiling in Iowa is lower than Minnesota's; the insurance industry employment ceiling in Des Moines is competitive with any comparable market in the Midwest. A candidate who is uncertain whether they want field production versus industry employment has strong reasons to consider Des Moines specifically — the density of insurance carrier employers creates optionality that Minneapolis-St. Paul and Milwaukee cannot replicate at the same scale.

All three states — Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Iowa — offer genuinely viable insurance careers. The differences in income ceiling, commercial complexity, licensing structure, and agricultural opportunity are real but not absolute. The producer who builds the right relationships, develops genuine expertise in their regional market's core industries, and retains clients through disciplined service will earn strong income in any of these states. The analysis above identifies where that income ceiling is highest, where the commercial complexity creates the most durable competitive advantage for specialty expertise, and where the licensing and CE frameworks are most and least demanding. For most candidates already in Minnesota who are evaluating whether to stay, the answer is clear — Minnesota's combination of the nation's hardest property market, a Fortune 500 commercial economy, and a growing self-employed population that is systematically underinsured creates the highest-ceiling, most opportunity-dense insurance career environment in the upper Midwest.
