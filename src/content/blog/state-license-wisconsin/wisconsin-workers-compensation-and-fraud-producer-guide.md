Wisconsin's workers' compensation system creates specific producer obligations in one of the Midwest's most diverse industrial states. Wisconsin workers' comp is a private competitive market with the Wisconsin Compensation Rating Bureau (WCRB) setting rates — an independent bureau state like Indiana, but different from Missouri (NCCI). Wisconsin's workers' comp is administered by the DWD Worker's Compensation Division (not OCI), and its coverage trigger is set low enough to capture most employers: three or more employees, or a single employee once the employer pays $500 or more in combined gross wages in a calendar quarter. That is a materially broader net than Missouri's 5-or-more general threshold, but it is still a threshold — a very small Wisconsin employer can fall below both prongs, and telling that client they are legally required to buy a policy is a misstatement of Wisconsin law. Wisconsin's workers' comp rates are among the nation's highest — Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking put Wisconsin's index at $1.42 per $100 of payroll, about 130% of the study median and 7th highest of the 51 jurisdictions surveyed — creating both a larger premium market and a need for producers who can help employers achieve the best available pricing within the WCRB framework.

## Wisconsin Workers' Compensation Framework

Administered by: DWD — Worker's Compensation Division (Wisconsin Department of Workforce Development) — NOT OCI

P.O. Box 7901, Madison, WI 53707

(608) 266-3046

dwd.wisconsin.gov/wc

OCI role: Regulates WCRB and workers' comp insurance companies. The ~300 WC carriers in Wisconsin must be WCRB members (confirmed by OCI).

Wisconsin Workers' Compensation Act: Wis. Stat. Chapter 102 (adopted 1911)

Coverage thresholds (Wis. Stat. § 102.04(1)(b); DWD publication WKC-13328-P) — an employer becomes subject to the Act when either prong is met:

3 or more employees: An employer that usually employs three or more full-time or part-time employees must have coverage **on the day it employs the third person**.

1 or more employees + $500 in a quarter: An employer with one or more full-time or part-time employees that pays combined gross wages of $500 or more in any calendar quarter for work done in Wisconsin must have coverage **by the 10th day of the first month of the next calendar quarter**.

Farm employers: A farmer who employs six or more workers on the same day for any 20 days during a calendar year must have coverage by the 10th day after that 20th day of employment.

What this means in practice: the $500-per-quarter prong is low enough that most Wisconsin employers with any payroll are captured quickly — but a one- or two-employee operation that stays under $500 in combined gross wages for the quarter is not required to carry coverage. Do not tell a small-employer client they are legally obligated to buy a policy before you have run both prongs against their actual payroll.

Domestic servants

Farm employees of a farmer below the 6-workers/20-days threshold

Members of religious sects with qualifying exemption

Native American tribal enterprises

Private competitive market: ~300 insurance companies licensed to write WC in Wisconsin — all must be WCRB members. Private carriers compete for accounts; producers earn commission on WC placements.

WCRB (Wisconsin Compensation Rating Bureau):

Independent bureau state — not NCCI

Private, nonprofit, unincorporated association of all WC insurers licensed in Wisconsin

Regulated by OCI; wcrb.org

Sets classification system, statistical plan, experience rating

Wisconsin's index rate was $1.42 per $100 of payroll in Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking — 130% of the study median and 7th highest of 51 jurisdictions, the inverse of Indiana, which ranked 45th of 51 (7th lowest) that year

TTD benefits: Two-thirds of the employee's average weekly earnings, subject to a statutory maximum that DWD resets each year. The maximum weekly rate for temporary total disability, permanent total disability, and death benefits is **$1,375 for injuries occurring on or after January 1, 2026** (computed on a statewide average weekly wage of $2,062.50); it was $1,326 for 2025 injuries. Because the applicable maximum is fixed by the date of injury, always confirm the rate for the injury year in DWD's Maximum Wage and Rate Chart (WKC-9572-P).

Exclusive remedy: Chapter 102 — employees cannot sue covered employers in tort for covered work-related injuries.

Self-insurance: Available with DWD approval.

## Wisconsin Workers' Comp by Industry

Dairy and agricultural sector: Wisconsin's dairy industry — the nation's leading cheese producer — creates workers' comp advisory for dairy farm operations, agricultural cooperatives, and food processing facilities. Dairy farms above the agricultural exemption threshold need workers' comp for farm employees. Agricultural processing (cheese plants, feed mills, cooperative grain elevators) are significant commercial workers' comp accounts.

Manufacturing (Oshkosh Corporation, Briggs & Stratton, Snap-on, SC Johnson supply chain): Wisconsin's manufacturing sector — specialty vehicles, consumer products, professional tools — creates significant workers' comp advisory throughout the state:

Oshkosh Corporation (Oshkosh): Fortune 500 military vehicle and specialty truck manufacturer; significant manufacturing workforce

Briggs & Stratton (Wauwatosa): Engine manufacturer; manufacturing workers

Snap-on (Kenosha): Professional tools; manufacturing and distribution workers

SC Johnson (Racine): Consumer products; manufacturing and packaging operations

Thousands of Wisconsin manufacturers throughout the Fox Valley, Milwaukee metro, and Racine/Kenosha corridor

Healthcare (Froedtert Health, UW Health, Aurora Health Care/Advocate Aurora, Children's Wisconsin): Wisconsin's major health systems employ tens of thousands — nurses, therapists, technicians, and support staff with healthcare-specific WC exposures (patient handling back injuries, needle sticks, exposure risks).

Construction: Wisconsin's construction sector — both commercial development and residential building — creates active workers' comp advisory. Construction wage levels mean a contractor with a single employee typically clears the $500-per-quarter threshold almost immediately, so in practice nearly every Wisconsin contractor with payroll needs coverage — and needs it by the 10th day of the following quarter.

Tourism and recreation: Wisconsin's tourism economy — Wisconsin Dells, Door County, Lake Geneva, Northwoods resorts — creates seasonal workers' comp advisory for hospitality and recreation employers.

## Wisconsin Insurance Fraud Framework

Producer anti-fraud obligations:

Complete applications accurately; never facilitate misrepresentation

Workers' comp: never assist employers in misclassifying employees to reduce premiums

No unauthorized signatures; no false documents

Cooperate with OCI investigations

Workers' comp fraud in Wisconsin: Wisconsin's private competitive market and high rates — 7th highest of 51 jurisdictions in Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking — create both employer fraud (payroll understatement; misclassification) and claimant fraud incentives. WCRB audit processes verify payrolls annually.

Agricultural exemption boundary fraud: Wisconsin's agricultural exemptions create a specific fraud risk: employers who misclassify non-exempt workers as farm laborers to avoid WC coverage. Producers who knowingly facilitate this misclassification face OCI enforcement action.

Construction subcontractor fraud: Wisconsin construction general contractors who fail to verify subcontractor WC coverage may become statutory employers for uninsured subcontractor employee injuries. Implement certificate of insurance verification systems for all Wisconsin construction accounts.

## 5 Frequently Asked Questions

- Why are Wisconsin's workers' comp rates among the highest in the nation? Wisconsin's index rate was $1.42 per $100 of payroll in Oregon DCBS's 2024 premium rate ranking — about 130% of the study median and 7th highest of 51 jurisdictions. Those rates reflect multiple factors: the WCRB's independent rate-setting (not NCCI's national competitive pressure), Wisconsin's manufacturing and agricultural risk profile, historical claims experience, and a low coverage threshold that pulls a broad premium base — including many higher-risk small employers — into the system. Despite above-median rates, Wisconsin has a competitive private market with ~300 carriers — producers can still help employers find the most competitive carrier pricing within the WCRB framework.
- What is the significance of Wisconsin's coverage threshold for producers? Wisconsin's two-pronged trigger — three or more employees, or one or more employees with $500 or more in combined gross quarterly wages — is low enough to create one of the broadest WC advisory markets among the comparison states. Missouri's general rule is 5+ employees, so a Missouri employer with 2-4 employees is outside the mandate while a comparable Wisconsin employer usually is not. That said, "low threshold" is not "no threshold": a Wisconsin employer with one or two employees who stays under $500 in a quarter and does not usually employ three people is not required to carry coverage. The practical advisory market is still large — small businesses, dairy farms above the 6-workers/20-days farm threshold, retail operations, and seasonal employers.
- How does the WCRB affect Wisconsin workers' comp advisory vs. NCCI states? Wisconsin's WCRB classification system may differ from NCCI class codes used in Missouri and most other states. A Wisconsin manufacturing operation may have a different WCRB code than the same operation would receive in Missouri under NCCI. Producers who specialize in Wisconsin WC need WCRB-specific classification knowledge — not just generic NCCI codes. The WCRB reference library at wcrb.org provides class code guidance.
- What happens to Wisconsin workers' comp claims after the DWD receives a dispute? The Wisconsin DWD Worker's Compensation Division adjudicates WC disputes through an administrative process. Workers' compensation judges (ALJs within DWD) hear contested claims. Appeals go to the Labor and Industry Review Commission (LIRC) and then to circuit court. OCI regulates the insurers but DWD manages the claims and dispute adjudication — a separation of administrative functions important for producers to understand when advising employers on claims management.
- Is the exclusive remedy doctrine (Chapter 102) absolute in Wisconsin? Workers' comp is the exclusive remedy for covered work-related injuries — injured employees cannot sue their Wisconsin employer in tort for covered injuries. Exceptions include intentional tortious acts by employers, cases where the injury is not "arising out of" employment, and third-party actions (suing non-employers who contributed to the injury). The exclusive remedy protects Wisconsin employers from civil lawsuit exposure for covered claims — the primary insurance mechanism for work-related injuries.
