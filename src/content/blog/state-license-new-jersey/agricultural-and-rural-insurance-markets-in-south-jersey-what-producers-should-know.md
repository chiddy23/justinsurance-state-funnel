New Jersey's "Garden State" moniker is not a marketing slogan — it is a functional description of an agricultural economy that generated approximately $1.5 billion in annual revenue from nearly 10,000 farms spread across 750,000 acres. Agriculture is the third-largest industry in the state, behind pharmaceuticals and tourism. The overwhelming majority of that agricultural activity is concentrated in South Jersey — Salem, Cumberland, Atlantic, Gloucester, Burlington, and Ocean counties — where the flat, sandy soils of the Outer Coastal Plain are suited to the specialty crop production that defines NJ's agricultural profile. For P&C producers who understand farm coverage, crop insurance, agribusiness commercial lines, and the rural community market, South Jersey represents a niche with strong client loyalty and genuinely limited competition from generic commercial lines operations.

### What New Jersey Grows: The Agricultural Profile

New Jersey is not a grain state. Unlike the Midwest's commodity agriculture, NJ's farming is almost entirely specialty crop and horticulture production — high-value, labor-intensive crops that require careful management and carry unique insurance exposures:

Blueberries: NJ is the national leader in blueberry production. Atlantic County (particularly the area around Hammonton) is the blueberry capital of the world, and the crop generates tens of millions of dollars annually. Blueberry operations require specific crop insurance — USDA RMA Actual Production History (APH) policies — and are vulnerable to frost, drought, and disease pressure.

Cranberries: NJ is a significant cranberry producer, concentrated in Ocean County's Pine Barrens. Cranberry farming requires specialized bog infrastructure that has specific commercial property and equipment breakdown insurance implications.

Tomatoes: NJ tomatoes are nationally recognized for quality, with the Jersey Fresh brand one of the oldest state agriculture marketing programs in the country. Tomato production is concentrated in Salem and Cumberland counties.

Nursery, greenhouse, and sod: The single largest sector of NJ's agricultural economy, generating approximately $500 million annually. Greenhouse and nursery operations represent some of the highest per-acre insurance values in the state — growing structures, specialized equipment, and high-value plant inventory all require specific commercial coverage.

Equine: New Jersey has a significant equine industry — breeding, racing (historically centered on the Meadowlands), boarding, and competition. Horse farms across Monmouth, Morris, Somerset, and Hunterdon counties represent a distinct agricultural insurance niche with specialized equine liability and mortality coverage needs.

Poultry, dairy, and field crops: Southern NJ's Salem and Cumberland counties support poultry and egg production. Dairy operations exist in the rural northern and western counties.

### The Insurance Products Farm Operations Need

#### Farm Package Policies

A farm package policy is the foundational coverage for most NJ farm operations. Unlike commercial property coverage for urban businesses, farm package policies are specifically designed for agricultural exposures. A typical NJ farm package includes:

Farmowners dwelling coverage — the farmhouse, often an older structure with specific valuation challenges

Farm structures — barns, equipment sheds, greenhouse structures, irrigation infrastructure, cold storage facilities, processing buildings

Farm personal property — farm machinery and equipment (tractors, harvesters, irrigation systems), livestock, grain and crops in storage, tools and supplies

Farm liability — broader than standard homeowners liability, covering farming operations, agritourism activities, and the use of farm machinery on public roads

Farm auto — specialized commercial auto coverage for farm tractors, trucks, and other equipment operating on and off the farm

#### Crop Insurance Through USDA RMA

Federal crop insurance is available through the USDA's Risk Management Agency (RMA) and is delivered exclusively through private crop insurance agents. All multi-peril crop insurance — including Catastrophic Risk Protection (CAT) policies — must be purchased through licensed private agents. This creates a specific advisory role for NJ P&C producers who hold appropriate crop insurance certifications.

For NJ's specialty crops, the primary crop insurance products include:

Actual Production History (APH) policies for blueberries, cranberries, tomatoes, and other covered crops

Revenue Protection (RP) policies that cover both yield loss and revenue decline

Whole Farm Revenue Protection (WFRP) for diversified farms with multiple crops

Crop insurance sign-up deadlines, acreage reporting requirements, and claim procedures are all regulated through USDA's Farm Service Agency and require agent expertise that goes beyond general P&C knowledge.

#### Greenhouse and Nursery Coverage

NJ's dominant horticultural sector — nearly $500 million in annual sales — requires greenhouse and nursery coverage that is not well-served by standard commercial property forms. Specialty coverage for growing structures, boiler and machinery for HVAC systems critical to plant survival, inland marine coverage for plants in transit, and business income coverage for seasonal operations are all components of a well-structured horticultural operation insurance program.

#### Agritourism Liability

Agritourism — pick-your-own berry farms, pumpkin patches, hayrides, corn mazes, farm stands, and seasonal events — has grown significantly as NJ farms diversify their revenue. New Jersey has a Right to Farm Act that provides some liability protection for agritourism activities, but producers offering these experiences still need specific agritourism liability endorsements. A hayride accident or pick-your-own injury creates real liability exposure that standard farm liability policies may not fully address without specific endorsements.

### The Rural Community Market: Beyond the Farm

South Jersey's rural communities are not exclusively agricultural — they are small-town communities with small businesses, homeowners, and personal lines needs that are often underserved by insurance operations concentrated in the urban and suburban north.

Rural small business: Salem and Cumberland counties support food processing operations, agricultural supply businesses, rural retail, and contractor operations that are commercial insurance prospects without the competition density of the northern NJ market.

Rural homeowners: The housing stock in rural South Jersey includes older homes on large parcels with outbuildings, livestock, and agricultural equipment — coverage that requires thoughtful placement beyond standard homeowners products.

Workers' compensation for agricultural employers: Farms with employees — particularly those using seasonal agricultural workers — require workers' compensation coverage. NJ's broad definition of employee and its strict classification tests apply to agricultural operations as well as urban businesses.

### Frequently Asked Questions

What makes New Jersey's agricultural market distinctive compared to other states' farm insurance markets?

New Jersey's agricultural market is almost entirely specialty crop and horticulture production rather than commodity row crops. Blueberries, cranberries, tomatoes, and greenhouse/nursery products are the dominant agricultural products — all high-value, labor-intensive crops with insurance needs that differ significantly from corn, wheat, and soybean coverage that dominates farm insurance in Midwest states. NJ's proximity to 50 million consumers along the Eastern Seaboard means that fresh produce can move from farm to consumer in under a day, which supports premium pricing but also creates pressure to deliver product when a crop fails. The combination of high crop values per acre, greenhouse infrastructure worth hundreds of thousands of dollars, and agritourism liability creates a complex insurance portfolio that rewards producers with agricultural specialty knowledge.

How does crop insurance work in New Jersey, and what is the producer's role?

Federal crop insurance through USDA's Risk Management Agency (RMA) is available only through private licensed agents — farmers cannot purchase it directly from USDA. All multi-peril crop insurance policies, including Catastrophic Risk Protection, must be placed through licensed private crop insurance agents. This means producers who complete RMA's crop insurance agent training and obtain appropriate crop insurance authorization have access to a federally-supported market that requires producer involvement at every stage — from pre-season sales and acreage reporting through claim adjusting. NJ's specialty crops — blueberries, cranberries, tomatoes, and various vegetables — are covered under USDA's specialty crop programs with their own sign-up deadlines, production history requirements, and coverage structures that reward agents with deep crop insurance expertise.

What is agritourism liability and why does it matter for NJ farm operations?

Agritourism describes farm activities that invite the public onto agricultural property for experiences like pick-your-own harvesting, pumpkin patches, corn mazes, hayrides, farm dinners, and seasonal festivals. New Jersey farms have rapidly expanded agritourism as a revenue diversification strategy, particularly as direct-to-consumer relationships grow stronger. New Jersey's Right to Farm Act (N.J.S.A. 4:1C-1 et seq.) provides some liability protections for agricultural operations, but the protections are not unlimited and do not replace the need for specific agritourism liability coverage. A hayride accident, a pick-your-own injury, a food safety incident at a farm market, or a visitor injured on farm equipment creates real liability exposure. Agritourism-specific endorsements or standalone agritourism liability policies address these exposures in ways that standard farm liability coverage often does not.

Are workers' compensation requirements different for NJ farm employers?

No — New Jersey's workers' compensation requirements apply to agricultural employers the same as any other employer. Coverage is required as soon as an agricultural operation has one employee — including seasonal workers. NJ's Workers' Compensation Act specifically covers agricultural workers, domestic workers, and unlawfully employed workers, meaning the broad coverage mandate applies fully to farm operations. The common practice of hiring seasonal farm workers in NJ — for blueberry harvesting, greenhouse work, or other labor-intensive operations — creates workers' compensation obligations that some farm operators may not be fully aware of. The penalties for non-compliance are the same as for any employer: up to $5,000 per 10-day uninsured period, personal liability for officers, and the Uninsured Employers Fund pursuing the employer for any paid claims.

What carrier options exist for farm insurance in New Jersey?

NJ farm operations can access farm package policies through national agricultural specialty carriers that write farm coverage in the Northeast — companies with specific expertise in farm risks and their unique exposures. For crop insurance, USDA's Risk Management Agency approves specific insurance companies to write federally reinsured crop insurance in NJ; the list of approved companies is published on RMA's website. Greenhouse and nursery operations often need specialty underwriters familiar with controlled-environment agriculture. Equine operations require carriers with equine mortality, medical, and liability programs. The competitive nature of NJ's commercial insurance market — over 400 carriers authorized in the state — means that most agricultural insurance needs can be met through the voluntary market, with the NJCRIB assigned risk pool available for workers' compensation accounts that cannot find voluntary market coverage.
